% Paragraph 2 — Problem statement (expanded: the data gap)
No single existing dataset satisfies these requirements simultaneously. Real-world scene datasets such as ScanNet~\cite{dai_scannet_2017} and Matterport3D~\cite{chang_matterport3d_2017} provide richly annotated indoor environments, but the occluded surfaces that completion methods must reconstruct are by definition absent from the captured data, preventing direct supervised evaluation of completion quality. Synthetic scene datasets such as SUNCG~\cite{song_semantic_2016} and Replica~\cite{straub_replica_2019} can provide complete geometry, but they deliver idealised outputs rather than sensor-dependent partial observations, omitting the noise, density variation and viewpoint-specific occlusion patterns that real scanners produce. At object level, ShapeNet~\cite{chang_shapenet_2015} offers scale and diversity but consists of CAD models that bear little resemblance to scanned data, while real scanned repositories such as Redwood~\cite{choi_large_2016} remain limited in size and category coverage.
 
An additional gap exists for the alignment and updating stage. Evaluating multi-temporal registration requires repeated captures of the same environment across time and across sensing technologies, yet most public datasets capture each scene only once and with a single device. Datasets that do provide temporal variation, such as 3RScan~\cite{wald_rio_2019}, use a single sensor type, while multi-sensor datasets such as ScanNet++ capture at a single time point. No public resource combines both axes of variation. Finally, industrial object categories such as piping, ductwork and storage vessels are absent from most standard benchmarks, leaving a blind spot in object completion evaluation for AECO applications.
 
% Paragraph 3 — Scope and contribution
To address these gaps, the chapter assembles three complementary data sources. Public datasets (Section~\ref{sec:3_existing}) provide established benchmarks for training and qualitative evaluation. Real-world multi-temporal captures from the KU~Leuven campus and a residential renovation site in Bruges (Section~\ref{sec:3_realworld}) supply the multi-sensor, multi-epoch data required for alignment evaluation, together with a dedicated industrial object dataset derived from the campus chemistry building. Finally, The V-Scan synthetic dataset (Section~\ref{sec:3_synthetic}) is introduced, filling the gap between real and synthetic data by combining a procedural room generator and configurable virtual scanner, providing partial scans paired with complete scene- and object-level ground truth under controlled conditions.

\section{Existing Datasets}
\label{sec:3_existing}

Public datasets are grouped into two categories: scene-level datasets, which provide scanned indoor environments, and object-level datasets, which provide isolated complete object models or scans.

% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\subsection{Scene-Level Datasets}
\label{sec:3_scene_datasets}

Large-scale real-world indoor datasets have played a central role in advancing scene understanding and reconstruction. Early datasets such as \textit{SUN RGB-D}~\cite{song_sun_2015} provided RGB-D images with room layout and object annotations, while \textit{Matterport3D}~\cite{chang_matterport3d_2017}, \textit{ScanNet}~\cite{dai_scannet_2017}, and \textit{Stanford 2D-3D-S}~\cite{armeni_joint_2017} introduced richly annotated indoor environments with registered imagery, depth data and reconstructed geometry. More recent datasets such as \textit{ARKitScenes}~\cite{baruch_arkitscenes_2022} and \textit{ScanNet++}~\cite{yeshwanth_scannet_2023} further improved scene diversity and geometric quality through mobile LiDAR and refined capture pipelines. These datasets have been highly valuable for semantic segmentation, scene reconstruction and 3D perception, but none provide ground truth for occluded regions, preventing direct supervised evaluation of completion quality.

\begin{figure}[h]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=1\textwidth]{Scannet++}
  \caption[ScanNet++ Dataset]{The ScanNet++ dataset, containing more than 1{,}000 indoor scenes. From \cite{yeshwanth_scannet_2023}.}
  \label{fig:3_ScanNet}
  \vspace{-10pt}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{ScanNet++.}
ScanNet++~\cite{yeshwanth_scannet_2023} is the first primary real-world dataset used for qualitative evaluation of our pipeline. It extends earlier RGB-D indoor datasets such as ScanNet~\cite{dai_scannet_2017} through significantly higher geometric and visual fidelity. Each scene is captured using a combination of terrestrial laser scans, registered 33-megapixel DSLR imagery and commodity RGB-D streams from mobile devices. This multi-modal acquisition strategy provides both highly accurate reference geometry and realistic lower-cost sensor observations within the same environments (Figure~\ref{fig:3_ScanNet}).

\paragraph{Matterport3D.}
Matterport3D~\cite{chang_matterport3d_2017} is the second primary real-world dataset used for qualitative evaluation. Captures were obtained using the Matterport Pro camera system and include globally aligned camera poses, textured meshes and semantic annotations (Figure~\ref{fig:3_Matterport3D}). Each environment is subdivided into multiple rooms, resulting in a large variety of bedrooms, living rooms, kitchens, hallways, offices and commercial interiors, making Matterport3D particularly useful for evaluating completion methods under realistic room layouts and varied furnishing styles.

\begin{figure}[h]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=1\textwidth]{Matterport3D}
  \caption[Matterport3D Dataset]{Example scenes from the Matterport3D dataset, containing 90 building-scale environments comprising approximately 10{,}800 panoramic viewpoints for color, depth and semantic annotations. From \cite{chang_matterport3d_2017}.}
  \label{fig:3_Matterport3D}
\end{figure}

Together, ScanNet++ and Matterport3D provide two complementary real-world benchmarks: one prioritising geometric fidelity and sensor diversity, the other prioritising spatial diversity and room-scale complexity.

% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\subsection{Object-Level Datasets}
\label{sec:3_object_datasets}

Datasets such as the \textit{Redwood Object Scan Dataset}~\cite{choi_large_2016}, \textit{Pix3D}~\cite{sun_pix3d_2018}, \textit{Google Scanned Objects}~\cite{downs_google_2022} and \textit{OmniObject3D}~\cite{wu_omniobject3d_2023} provide high-quality 3D object models derived from real scanning or image-based reconstruction. These resources are valuable for object reconstruction, novel-view synthesis and shape modelling, but remain limited in scale, diversity and annotation quality compared to synthetic repositories like ShapeNet~\cite{chang_shapenet_2015}.

\paragraph{ShapeNet.}
ShapeNet~\cite{chang_shapenet_2015} is the first primary dataset used for quantitative isolated object completion experiments in Chapter~\ref{ch:7_Object_Completion}. It is a large-scale repository of CAD-based 3D object models organised into semantic categories, containing tens of thousands of examples across common furniture and household object classes (Figure~\ref{fig:3_ShapeNet}). Its principal strength lies in the availability of clean and complete geometry, making it well suited for controlled benchmarking and for training data-hungry completion networks.

\begin{figure}[h]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=1\textwidth]{Shapenet}
  \caption[ShapeNet Dataset]{A selection of objects from the ShapeNet~\cite{chang_shapenet_2015} dataset, consisting of over 51{,}300 models covering 55 categories.}
  \label{fig:3_ShapeNet}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{Redwood.}
The Redwood 3D Scan Dataset~\cite{choi_large_2016} is the second primary dataset used for quantitative isolated object completion evaluation. It contains real objects captured with consumer RGB-D sensors and reconstructed into dense 3D models (Figure~\ref{fig:3_Redwood}). Despite its smaller scale compared to ShapeNet, Redwood reflects the imperfections of real-world acquisition, including sensor noise, incomplete observations and reconstruction artefacts, making it particularly valuable for assessing whether methods trained on synthetic CAD models remain effective on realistic scanned objects.
\begin{figure}[h]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=1\textwidth]{Redwood}
  \caption[Redwood Dataset]{Example scenes from the Redwood dataset~\cite{choi_large_2016}, consisting of 441 real objects captured with consumer RGB-D sensors and reconstructed into dense 3D models.}
  \label{fig:3_Redwood}
\end{figure}

Together, ShapeNet and Redwood provide complementary object-level benchmarks: ShapeNet offers scale and clean geometry, while Redwood offers realism and sensor-derived shape variation.

\section{Real-World Data Capture}
\label{sec:3_realworld}

Real-world captures are performed at multiple sites on the KU~Leuven Technology Campus in Ghent and at a residential renovation site in Bruges, using a combination of high-end terrestrial laser scanners and consumer-grade \gls{XR} devices. The resulting collection (Table~\ref{tab:3_captured_scenes_metrics}) spans three categories of indoor environments and more than three orders of magnitude in per-session point count, providing the multi-temporal and multi-sensory data required for the alignment experiments in Chapter~\ref{ch:4_Alignment_Updating}. In addition, a dedicated industrial object dataset is derived from scans and \gls{BIM} modelling of the campus chemistry building (Section~\ref{sec:3_chemistry}), supplying ground-truth geometry for industrial piping and vessels that are absent from public object-level benchmarks.
 
The sensors used to capture this data are described in Section~\ref{sec:3_sensors}, the multi-temporal environments in Section~\ref{sec:3_captured_scenes}, and the industrial object dataset in Section~\ref{sec:3_chemistry}.

\paragraph{Terrestrial Laser Scanners.}
Static \gls{TLS} systems such as the Leica ScanStation P30~\cite{noauthor_leica_nodate} and BLK360~\cite{noauthor_leica_nodate-1} provide the highest measurement accuracy, typically achieving single-point accuracies between 1 and 2\,mm for the P30 and 5 to 10\,mm for the BLK360 at ranges up to 100 and 60\,m respectively~\cite{bassier_standalone_2016}. These systems capture structured point grids of 10 to 40 million points per setup, along with panoramic imagery. However, the static nature of the measurement introduces significant occlusions at ground level and behind objects, as only surfaces visible from the scanner position are recorded. Consecutive setups must be co-registered, typically through a combination of target-based and cloud-to-cloud methods, with millimetre level error propagation achievable across setups~\cite{geyter_point_2022}.

\paragraph{Indoor Mobile Mapping Systems.}
Indoor mobile mapping systems (\gls{iMMS}) such as the NavVis VLX~\cite{noauthor_navvis_nodate} combine LiDAR sensors with camera-based \gls{SLAM} algorithms to achieve continuous data acquisition while the operator moves through the environment. These systems produce unordered point clouds with uneven point distributions, but their continuous trajectory results in significantly better spatial coverage than static scanners, particularly in cluttered environments. Global accuracies of LOA20 ($2\sigma \leq 0.05$\,m) to LOA30 ($2\sigma \leq 0.015$\,m) are achievable when supported by control measurements~\cite{geyter_point_2022, vermandere_two-step_2022}. Data acquisition is typically two to three times faster than with static \gls{TLS}.

\paragraph{XR Devices.}
Extended Reality devices such as the Microsoft HoloLens~2~\cite{hubner_evaluation_2020} represent the lower end of the capture spectrum. These head-mounted devices are equipped with a 1\,MP \gls{ToF} sensor and four grey-scale tracking cameras, producing real-time polygonal meshes at a default spatial resolution of approximately 0.08\,m$^3$~\cite{geyter_point_2022, hubner_evaluation_2020}. While their accuracy is limited, with single-point accuracies of approximately 1\,cm/m and a maximum useful range of 3 to 4\,m, they offer unparalleled mobility and can capture regions inaccessible to larger systems. The accompanying RGB camera provides localised imagery that can be used for 2D-based alignment methods.

\paragraph{Smartphone LiDAR.}
Recent smartphones equipped with LiDAR sensors, such as iPhone models with the Apple LiDAR Scanner~\cite{abdel-majeed_indoor_2024}, provide another commodity-level capture modality. These devices produce dense point clouds at close range with moderate accuracy, and their widespread availability makes them relevant for incremental updating scenarios where dedicated scanning equipment is not practical.

\subsection{Captured Environments}
\label{sec:3_captured_scenes}

In each environment every sensor is deployed according to its intended use case, with \gls{TLS} setups placed to maximise structural coverage, \gls{iMMS} captures following natural walking trajectories with loop closures where possible, and \gls{XR} captures focused on cluttered regions inaccessible to the larger systems~\cite{geyter_point_2022}.

\paragraph{Laboratory Rooms.} The laboratory rooms (Figure~\ref{fig:3_campus_dataset}) are single-storey industrial spaces containing working desks, test equipment and machinery. Between captures taken several months apart, all movable objects are displaced in the course of ongoing research activity, making these rooms the primary test case for multi-temporal alignment under layout change in an otherwise stable structural envelope.

\subsection{Industrial Object Dataset}
\label{sec:3_chemistry}

The public object-level datasets described in Section~\ref{sec:3_object_datasets} primarily cover common residential furniture categories such as chairs, tables and sofas. Industrial objects, including piping, ductwork and storage vessels, are largely absent from both ShapeNet and Redwood yet are central to the AECO applications targeted in this thesis. To fill this gap, a dedicated industrial object dataset is created from the chemistry building (M-blok) on the KU~Leuven campus in Ghent (Figure~\ref{fig:3_chemistry_building}).

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=1\textwidth]{Chemistry_Dataset}
  \caption[Chemistry building dataset]{The industrial object dataset derived from the chemistry building. Left: the full Revit model of the pilot brewery. Top right: isolated rectangular pipe sections. Bottom right: isolated round pipe sections.}
  \label{fig:3_chemistry_building}

The building houses a pilot brewery containing a variety of round and rectangular piping sections connected through bends, junctions and transitions, as well as storage vessels of different sizes and shapes. The environment is scanned using a Leica ScanStation P30 and a Leica BLK360. From the captured point clouds, 22 distinct pipe configurations and 9 vessels are identified. An as-built \gls{BIM} model of the facility was subsequently created in Autodesk Revit, modelling the vessels and piping.

To complement the scanned objects with a broader set of controlled industrial geometry, 50 additional pipe elements are manually constructed as parametric Revit families. These cover rectangular and round cross-sections with varying bends, junctions and transitions, representing the most common configurations encountered in industrial indoor environments. Together with the 9 modelled vessels, the dataset comprises 59 synthetic industrial objects spanning three classes not present in standard benchmarks: round pipe sections, rectangular pipe sections, and industrial vessels. V-Scan is produced by a two-part framework (Figure~\ref{fig:3_VScan_Method}): a procedural room generator that produces varied indoor environments (Section~\ref{sec:3_room_generator}), and a virtual scanner that mimics real acquisition devices to scan them (Section~\ref{sec:3_virtual_scanner}). The remainder of this section first reviews related synthetic-data work (Section~\ref{sec:3_synthetic_related}) before detailing the framework and the dataset it produces.

\subsection{Related Work}
\label{sec:3_synthetic_related}

Synthetic datasets are widely used in 3D computer vision to overcome the cost, scale and annotation limitations of real-world data collection. Datasets such as \textit{SUNCG}~\cite{song_semantic_2016}, \textit{SceneNet RGB-D}~\cite{mccormac_scenenet_nodate}, \textit{InteriorNet}~\cite{li_interiornet_2018} and \textit{Replica}~\cite{straub_replica_2019} provide large collections of indoor environments with rendered imagery, semantic labels and complete scene geometry. However, they generally provide complete environments directly and do not model the partial, sensor-dependent observations encountered during real scanning.

Procedural scene generation offers a complementary approach. \textit{Infinigen Indoors}~\cite{raistrick_infinigen_nodate} generates photorealistic indoor spaces using constraint-based procedural rules, \textit{RoomPilot}~\cite{chen_roompilot_2025} produces structured layouts from textual descriptions, and \textit{DiffuScene}~\cite{tang_diffuscene_2024} applies diffusion models to synthesise plausible furniture arrangements. These works demonstrate increasing realism in synthetic scene creation, but are not intended for benchmark generation focused on viewpoint-dependent scanning and completion evaluation.

Several studies have explored virtual scanning and LiDAR simulation to bridge the gap between ideal geometry and measured data. Early work such as Blume~et~al.~\cite{blume_simulating_2006} focused on range sensing simulation for robotics. More recent systems, including Popovas~et~al.~\cite{popovas_virtual_2021} and \textit{HELIOS++}~\cite{winiwarter_virtual_2022}, incorporate viewpoint-dependent visibility, sampling behaviour and sensor noise with high realism, demonstrating the importance of modelling the acquisition process itself. However, these systems target surveying or robotics scenarios and do not provide object-level ground truth or completion-oriented supervision for furnished indoor environments.

The framework proposed here combines both directions, integrating procedural scene generation with configurable virtual scanning to produce partial observations together with complete scene- and object-level ground truth.

\subsection{Procedural Room Layout Generator}
\label{sec:3_room_generator}

A procedural scene generation system is developed to automatically create diverse indoor environments from a predefined library of structural and furniture elements (Figure~\ref{fig:3_Room_Generator}). Room and furniture generation are separated, enabling multiple temporal variants of the same room geometry with different object layouts. Each random sampling step is deterministic based on a saved seed, ensuring reproducibility.

\subsubsection{Prefab Selection}

Room generation is based on a collection of modular components together with generation parameters: grid size, furniture density, element probabilities and a list of thematically appropriate furniture.

\subsubsection{Room Structure Generation}

The room structure is generated on a grid-based system. Floor dimensions are randomly sampled within configurable bounds, and the floor, walls and ceiling are assembled from modular tiles. Each wall segment has a configurable probability of being replaced by a door or window element, and the room is retextured with a random colour and pattern to introduce visual variety.

\subsubsection{Furniture Placement}

Furniture placement is divided into wall-mounted and free-standing categories. Wall-mounted items are positioned against available wall surfaces, while free-standing objects are placed using rejection sampling: candidate positions and orientations are accepted only if their Oriented Bounding Boxes (\gls{OBB}s) do not intersect previously placed objects. The total number of objects is controlled by a density parameter proportional to room area.

Some examples of the generated room layouts are shown in Figure~\ref{fig:3_Procedural_Rooms}. The resulting scenes exhibit a wide variety of room sizes, colours and furniture arrangements, providing a base for the subsequent virtual scanning.

In addition to standard scene capture, the scanner supports automated object extraction, empty-scene rescanning and configurable scan variations for dataset generation.

\subsubsection{Scanner Configuration}

The scanner behaviour is controlled through a set of parameters describing the sensing characteristics of the simulated device (visualised in Figure~\ref{fig:3_VScan_Parameters}):\\
\begin{wrapfigure}{r}{0.34\textwidth}
  \centering
  \includegraphics[]{Scanner_params}
  \caption[V-Scan Parameters]{Overview of the V-Scan parameters.}
  \label{fig:3_VScan_Parameters}
\end{wrapfigure}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Scan density}: $\rho$, the angular spacing between consecutive rays, expressed as the point spacing in mm at 10\,m distance.
    \item \textbf{Scan range}: $r$, the maximum detection distance, in metres.
    \item \textbf{Vertical scan range}: $r_{\text{vert}}$, the total angular extent of the vertical field of view, in degrees. A value less than 360$^\circ$ simulates the blind spots typically found right under the scanner.
    \item \textbf{System noise}: $\sigma_{\text{sys}}$, the standard deviation of range-independent measurement noise, in mm.
    \item \textbf{Distance noise}: $\sigma_{\text{dist}}$, the standard deviation of range-dependent measurement noise, in mm per 10\,m.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Geometric Scanning}

The acquisition process is simulated by casting rays from the scanner origin into the scene according to a spherical sampling pattern. For an angular spacing $\rho$, the number of horizontal and vertical samples is given by:

\begin{equation}
n_{\text{horiz}} = \left\lceil \frac{2\pi}{\rho} \right\rceil,
\qquad
n_{\text{vert}} = \left\lceil \frac{\pi}{\rho} \right\rceil .
\end{equation}

Rays outside the selected vertical field of view are discarded to reproduce blind regions typically present above and below terrestrial scanners. Whenever a ray intersects a surface within range, a point is recorded together with its position, surface normal and texture coordinates. Optional Gaussian noise is added to the measured range:

\begin{equation}
d' = d + \mathcal{N}\left(0,\sigma_{\text{sys}} + \sigma_{\text{dist}}\, d \right),
\end{equation}
where:
\begin{itemize}
    \item $d$ is the true distance,
    \item $\sigma_{\text{sys}}$ is the constant noise term,
    \item $\sigma_{\text{dist}}$ controls the distance-dependent uncertainty.
\end{itemize}
After acquisition, points can be filtered to a predefined scan volume to retain only observations inside the target scene region (Figure~\ref{fig:3_vscan_scan_densities}).

\subsection{Dataset Generation Pipeline}
\label{sec:3_scan_management}
 
The procedural room generator and virtual scanner are coordinated by a \texttt{ScanManager} that orchestrates the automated generation of complete dataset entries. For each generated room, the scan manager executes the sequence summarised in Figure~\ref{fig:3_VScan_Pipeline}.

The variation pass in step 5 supports three independent axes: furniture layout while preserving the room structure, scanner preset to simulate different sensing devices, and scanner position within the room. Each variation is stored alongside the main scan with its acquisition parameters, and an arbitrary number of variation passes can be configured per room, enabling controlled experiments on the impact of individual factors.
 
For each generated scene, the dataset stores the scans together with the metadata and ground-truth annotations required for evaluation at scene level (furnished vs. \ empty scenes), object level (partially scanned objects vs.\ ground-truth meshes) and detection level (ground-truth bounding boxes) (Figure~\ref{fig:3_vscan_data}). Point clouds are exported in ASCII format with position, normal and colour attributes; panoramic images as PNG; complete object meshes as PLY; and bounding boxes, room parameters, scan settings and variation parameters as JSON (Figure~\ref{fig:3_json_formats}).

\subsection{Limitations}
\label{sec:3_vscan_limitations}
 
Two limitations of the V-Scan dataset should be noted. The procedural room generator currently supports only rectangular room layouts assembled from modular grid tiles, restricting the geometric complexity of the generated environments to orthogonal configurations. This is an intentional scoping decision that matches the planar assumption adopted by the scene completion stage in Chapter~\ref{ch:6_Scene_Completion}, but it means that more complex room layouts are not represented and can only be evaluated qualitatively using the existing scene-level datasets. A second limitation is the absence of complex lighting conditions. Every room is evenly lit without harsh lights or very dark areas. While this greatly improves clarity of the generated data, it may not always reflect real scanning conditions where shadows and uneven illumination affect both colour assignment and downstream texture processing.

\section{Conclusion}

This chapter defined the data acquisition strategy for the thesis, combining public datasets, real-world multi-temporal captures and a contributed synthetic generation framework to meet the evaluation requirements of the pipeline introduced in Chapter~\ref{ch:introduction}.

In addition to the existing public datasets, three data contributions were made. The real-world capture campaign produced 45 sessions across three indoor environments using four sensor classes (Table~\ref{tab:3_captured_scenes_metrics}), providing the heterogeneous multi-temporal input for alignment evaluation. The V-Scan generation framework produces 200 scene folders containing 3,100 objects (Table~\ref{tab:vscan_summary}), pairing partial scans with complete scene- and object-level ground truth, a combination that no existing dataset offers and that directly addresses the evaluation barrier identified in Chapter~\ref{ch:introduction}.

The V-Scan dataset is consumed by every subsequent experimental chapter for quantitative evaluation: alignment validation through known ground-truth poses, detection evaluation through bounding box annotations, scene completion evaluation through empty-scene geometry, and object completion evaluation through per-object partial scans and complete meshes. The chemistry building dataset complements these for the industrial object categories evaluated in Chapter~\ref{ch:7_Object_Completion}.
