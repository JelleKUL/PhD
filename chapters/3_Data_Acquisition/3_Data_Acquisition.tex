\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:3_intro}

This chapter outlines the data used throughout the thesis. The pipeline requires partial indoor scans with colour, repeated multi-sensor captures for alignment evaluation, object-level bounding box annotations, and ground-truth geometry for both scene and object completion. No single existing dataset satisfies all of these requirements simultaneously: real-world datasets lack ground truth for occluded regions, while synthetic repositories do not reproduce realistic scanning conditions. This thesis therefore combines three complementary sources: publicly available datasets (Section~\ref{sec:3_existing}), real-world multi-temporal captures (Section~\ref{sec:3_realworld}), and the novel V-Scan synthetic dataset (Section~\ref{sec:3_synthetic}).

% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Existing Datasets}
\label{sec:3_existing}

The public datasets are grouped in two categories: Scene-level datasets, typically providing scanned indoor environments, and object-level datasets, consisting of isolated complete object models or scans.

% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\subsection{Scene-Level Datasets}
\label{sec:3_scene_datasets}

Large-scale real-world indoor datasets have played a central role in advancing scene understanding and reconstruction. Early datasets such as \textit{SUN RGB-D}~\cite{song_sun_2015} provided RGB-D images with room layout and object annotations, while \textit{Matterport3D}~\cite{chang_matterport3d_2017}, \textit{ScanNet}~\cite{dai_scannet_2017}, and \textit{Stanford 2D-3D-S}~\cite{armeni_joint_2017} introduced richly annotated indoor environments with registered imagery, depth data, and reconstructed geometry. More recent datasets such as \textit{ARKitScenes}~\cite{baruch_arkitscenes_2022} and \textit{ScanNet++}~\cite{yeshwanth_scannet_2023} further improved scene diversity and geometric quality through mobile LiDAR and refined capture pipelines. These datasets have been highly valuable for semantic segmentation, scene reconstruction, and 3D perception, but none provide ground truth for occluded regions, preventing direct supervised evaluation of completion quality.

\paragraph{ScanNet++.}

ScanNet++~\cite{yeshwanth_scannet_2023} is one of the primary real-world datasets used for both quantitative and qualitative evaluation of the pipeline. It extends earlier RGB-D indoor datasets such as ScanNet~\cite{dai_scannet_2017} through significantly higher geometric and visual fidelity. The dataset contains more than 1,000 indoor scenes, each captured using a combination of sub-millimetre terrestrial laser scans, registered 33-megapixel DSLR imagery, and commodity RGB-D streams from mobile devices. This multi-modal acquisition strategy provides both highly accurate reference geometry and realistic lower-cost sensor observations within the same environments (Figure~\ref{fig:3_ScanNet}).

\paragraph{Matterport3D.}

Matterport3D~\cite{chang_matterport3d_2017} is the second primary real-world dataset used for evaluation. It contains 90 building-scale environments comprising approximately 10,800 panoramic viewpoints reconstructed from 194,400 RGB-D images. Captures were obtained using the Matterport Pro camera system and include globally aligned camera poses, textured meshes and semantic annotations (Figure~\ref{fig:3_Matterport3D}). Each environment is subdivided into multiple rooms, resulting in a large variety of bedrooms, living rooms, kitchens, hallways, offices and commercial interiors, making Matterport3D particularly useful for evaluating completion methods under realistic room layouts and varied furnishing styles.

Together, ScanNet++ and Matterport3D provide two complementary real-world benchmarks: one prioritising geometric fidelity and sensor diversity, the other prioritising spatial diversity and room-scale complexity.

\label{sec:3_realworld}

Real-world captures were performed at the KU~Leuven Technology Campus in Ghent across a range of industrial laboratory spaces and residential environments, using a combination of high-end terrestrial laser scanners and consumer-grade \gls{XR} devices.

\paragraph{Terrestrial Laser Scanners.}
Static \gls{TLS} systems such as the Leica ScanStation P30~\cite{noauthor_leica_nodate} and BLK360~\cite{noauthor_leica_nodate-1} provide the highest measurement accuracy, typically achieving single-point accuracies below 5\,mm at ranges up to 50\,m~\cite{bassier_standalone_2016}. These systems capture structured point grids of 10 to 40 million points per setup, along with panoramic imagery. However, the static nature of the measurement introduces significant occlusions at ground level and behind objects, as only surfaces visible from the scanner position are recorded. Consecutive setups must be registered, typically through a combination of target-based and cloud-to-cloud methods, with sub-millimetre error propagation achievable across setups~\cite{geyter_point_2022}.

\paragraph{Indoor Mobile Mapping Systems.}
Indoor mobile mapping systems (\gls{iMMS}) such as the NavVis VLX~\cite{noauthor_navvis_nodate} combine LiDAR sensors with camera-based \gls{SLAM} algorithms to achieve continuous data acquisition while the operator moves through the environment. These systems produce unordered point clouds with uneven point distributions, but their continuous trajectory results in significantly better spatial coverage than static scanners, particularly in cluttered environments. Global accuracies of LOA20 ($2\sigma \leq 0.05$\,m) to LOA30 ($2\sigma \leq 0.015$\,m) are achievable when supported by control measurements~\cite{geyter_point_2022, vermandere_two-step_2022}. Data acquisition is typically two to three times faster than with static \gls{TLS}.

\paragraph{XR Devices.}
Extended Reality devices such as the Microsoft HoloLens~2~\cite{hubner_evaluation_2020} represent the lower end of the capture spectrum. These head-mounted devices are equipped with a 1\,MP \gls{ToF} sensor and four grey-scale tracking cameras, producing real-time polygonal meshes at a default spatial resolution of approximately 0.08\,m$^3$~\cite{geyter_point_2022, hubner_evaluation_2020}. While their accuracy is limited, with single-point accuracies of approximately 1\,cm/m and a maximum useful range of 3--4\,m, they offer unparalleled mobility and can capture regions inaccessible to larger systems. The accompanying RGB camera provides localised imagery that can be used for 2D-based alignment methods.

\paragraph{Smartphone LiDAR.}
Recent smartphones equipped with LiDAR sensors, such as iPhone models with the Apple LiDAR Scanner~\cite{abdel-majeed_indoor_2024}, provide another commodity-level capture modality. These devices produce dense point clouds at close range with moderate accuracy, and their widespread availability makes them relevant for incremental updating scenarios where dedicated scanning equipment is not practical.

\subsection{Capture Methodology}
\label{sec:3_capture_method}

Each sensing technology was deployed according to its intended use case~\cite{geyter_point_2022}. For \gls{TLS} captures, one or two scanner setups were placed per room, positioning the scanner to maximise coverage of the structural elements while accepting the inherent ground-level occlusions caused by furniture and equipment. For \gls{iMMS} captures, the operator walked through the environment following a natural trajectory, ensuring loop closures where possible to minimise drift accumulation. For \gls{XR} device captures, the operator walked around the room attempting to capture all accessible surfaces, including regions underneath and behind furniture that are typically harder to reach with larger scanners.

This deliberate combination of sensors and deployment strategies produces complementary datasets: \gls{TLS} data provides high-accuracy structural geometry with systematic occlusions, \gls{iMMS} data provides high-coverage reconstructions with moderate accuracy, and \gls{XR} data provides fast, accessible captures with reduced accuracy but improved coverage in cluttered areas.

\subsection{Captured Scenes}
\label{sec:3_captured_scenes}

Three categories of indoor environments were captured across the KU~Leuven campus in Ghent and at a residential site in Bruges (Figure~\ref{fig:3_captured_scenes}). These comprise industrial laboratory rooms used for soil, asphalt, and building physics research, a prefab living lab constructed on the campus, and a four-storey residential renovation in Bruges. Each environment was documented at multiple time points and with multiple sensors, producing the multi-temporal and multi-sensory data required for the alignment experiments in Chapter~\ref{ch:4_Alignment_Updating}. Between capture epochs, the environments were in active use or under active construction, resulting in natural variations in object placement, surface treatment, and structural configuration.

The laboratory rooms are single-storey industrial spaces with footprints on the order of 10\,m~$\times$~30\,m, containing working desks, test equipment, machinery, and bulk materials. Between captures taken several months apart, all movable objects were displaced in the course of ongoing research activity, making these rooms the primary test case for multi-temporal alignment under layout change in an otherwise stable structural envelope.

The living lab is a three-storey prefab residential building erected on the campus as a dedicated research facility. It was captured repeatedly throughout its construction, from early stake-out through to the installation of mechanical, electrical, and plumbing (MEP) systems, providing test data in which both the geometry and the surface appearance change drastically between epochs.

The Bruges renovation is a four-storey residential building including a full basement and attic, captured during an interior refurbishment. The structural envelope is largely fixed, but the interior is cluttered with construction materials, tools, and temporary storage that change position between visits, complementing the living lab with a residential-scale renovation environment rather than a new-build construction site.

In total, 45 measurement sessions were recorded across the three sites using the full range of sensors introduced in Section~\ref{sec:3_sensors}~\cite{vermandere_two-step_2022}. \section{Synthetic Data Creation}
\label{sec:3_synthetic}

The real-world captures provide heterogeneous multi-sensory data for alignment evaluation, but lack complete geometric ground truth for quantitative assessment of scene and object completion. Surfaces that are occluded during acquisition are, by definition, absent from the captured data. A synthetic data generation framework was therefore developed to produce partial scans together with complete reference geometry under controlled conditions that approximate real indoor scanning scenarios.

\subsection{Related Work}
\label{sec:3_synthetic_related}

Synthetic datasets are widely used in 3D computer vision to overcome the cost, scale, and annotation limitations of real-world data collection. At the scene level, datasets such as \textit{SUNCG}~\cite{song_semantic_2016}, \textit{SceneNet RGB-D}~\cite{mccormac_scenenet_nodate}, \textit{InteriorNet}~\cite{li_interiornet_2018}, and \textit{Replica}~\cite{straub_replica_2019} provide large collections of indoor environments with rendered imagery, semantic labels, and complete scene geometry. However, they generally provide complete environments directly and do not model the partial, sensor-dependent observations encountered during real scanning.

At the object level, repositories such as \textit{ShapeNet}~\cite{chang_shapenet_2015}, \textit{ModelNet}~\cite{wu_3d_2015}, and \textit{Amazon Berkeley Objects}~\cite{collins_abo_2022} contain extensive collections of CAD models and scanned object instances valuable for classification, retrieval, and geometric learning. Nevertheless, they represent complete objects in isolation and do not capture the contextual occlusions typical of indoor scenes.

Several studies have explored virtual scanning and LiDAR simulation to bridge the gap between ideal geometry and measured data. Early work such as Blume~et~al.~\cite{blume_simulating_2006} focused on range sensing simulation for robotics. More recent systems, including Popovas~et~al.~\cite{popovas_virtual_2021} and \textit{HELIOS++}~\cite{winiwarter_virtual_2022}, incorporate viewpoint-dependent visibility, sampling behaviour, and sensor noise with high realism, demonstrating the importance of modelling the acquisition process itself. However, these systems target surveying or robotics scenarios and do not provide object-level ground truth or completion-oriented supervision for furnished indoor environments.

Procedural scene generation offers a complementary approach. \textit{Infinigen Indoors}~\cite{raistrick_infinigen_nodate} generates photorealistic indoor spaces using constraint-based procedural rules, \textit{RoomPilot}~\cite{chen_roompilot_2025} produces structured layouts from textual descriptions, and \textit{DiffuScene}~\cite{tang_diffuscene_2024} applies diffusion models to synthesise plausible furniture arrangements. These works demonstrate increasing realism in synthetic scene creation, but are not intended for benchmark generation focused on viewpoint-dependent scanning and completion evaluation.

The framework proposed here combines both directions, integrating procedural scene generation with configurable virtual scanning to produce partial observations together with complete scene- and object-level ground truth.

\subsection{Virtual Scanner}
\label{sec:3_virtual_scanner}

A virtual scanner was developed to simulate common indoor acquisition devices within a 3D environment. For each scan position, the system generates a coloured point cloud together with a panoramic image, approximating the outputs of terrestrial laser scanners and mobile depth-sensing devices. In addition to standard scene capture, the framework supports automated object extraction, empty-scene rescanning, and configurable scan variations for dataset generation. The full pipeline is illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:3_VScan_Method}.

\subsubsection{Scanner Configuration}

The scanner behaviour is controlled through a set of parameters describing the sensing characteristics of the simulated device:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Scan density}: the angular spacing between consecutive rays, expressed as the point spacing in mm at 10\,m distance.
    \item \textbf{Scan range}: the maximum detection distance, in metres.
    \item \textbf{Vertical scan range}: the total angular extent of the vertical field of view, in degrees. A value less than 360° simulates the blind spots typically found at the zenith and nadir of terrestrial laser scanners.
    \item \textbf{System noise}: $\sigma_{\text{sys}}$, the standard deviation of range-independent measurement noise, in mm.
    \item \textbf{Distance noise}: $\sigma_{\text{dist}}$, the standard deviation of range-dependent measurement noise, in mm per 10\,m.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Geometric Scanning}

The acquisition process is simulated by casting rays from the scanner origin into the scene according to a spherical sampling pattern. For an angular spacing $\delta$, the number of horizontal and vertical samples is given by:

\begin{equation}
n_{\text{horiz}} = \left\lceil \frac{2\pi}{\delta} \right\rceil,
\qquad
n_{\text{vert}} = \left\lceil \frac{\pi}{\delta} \right\rceil
\end{equation}

Rays outside the selected vertical field of view are discarded to reproduce blind regions typically present above and below terrestrial scanners. Whenever a ray intersects a surface within range, a point is recorded together with its position, surface normal, and texture coordinates. Optional Gaussian noise is added to the measured range:

\begin{equation}
d' = d + \mathcal{N}\left(0,\sigma_{\text{sys}} + \sigma_{\text{dist}} d \right)
\end{equation}

where $d$ is the true distance, $\sigma_{\text{sys}}$ is the constant noise term, and $\sigma_{\text{dist}}$ controls distance-dependent uncertainty. After acquisition, points can be filtered to a predefined scan volume to retain only observations inside the target scene region.

\subsubsection{Image Capture and Colour Assignment}

Colour information is captured through a spherical image render at the sensor location. The camera renders an equirectangular panoramic image, which serves both as the panoramic image output and as the source for point cloud colouring. Each scanned point is assigned a colour by sampling the panoramic image at its corresponding \gls{UV} coordinates derived from the angles of the cast rays.

\subsubsection{Object Isolation}

Each object instance in the scene is associated with an oriented bounding box \gls{OBB}, used to isolate the scanned points that fall within it and obtain partial object observations. For every object, the framework stores the isolated partial point cloud, the complete reference mesh, and the oriented bounding box parameters.

\subsubsection{Scan Variations}

To support robustness analysis, additional scans can be generated by modifying selected scene or sensor conditions. Three variation types are supported: (i)~changes in furniture layout while preserving the room structure, (ii)~changes in scanner preset to simulate different sensing devices, and (iii)~changes in scanner position within the room. Each variation is exported as an additional scan together with its corresponding acquisition parameters, enabling controlled comparison of reconstruction performance under changing conditions.

\subsubsection{Export Format}

Point clouds are exported in ASCII format containing position, normal, and colour attributes. Complete object meshes are stored in PLY format, while metadata such as bounding boxes, room parameters, scan settings, and variation parameters are stored as JSON files.

\subsection{Procedural Room Layout Generator}
\label{sec:3_room_generator}

A procedural scene generation system was developed to automatically create diverse indoor environments from a predefined library of structural and furniture elements (Figure \ref{fig:3_Room_Generator}). Room and furniture generation are separated, enabling multiple temporal variants of the same room geometry with different object layouts. Each random sampling step is deterministic based on a saved seed, ensuring reproducibility.

\subsubsection{Prefab Selection}

Room generation is based on a collection of modular components together with generation parameters: grid size, furniture density, and element probabilities, and a list of thematically appropriate furniture.

\subsubsection{Room Structure Generation}

The room structure is generated on a grid-based system. Floor dimensions are randomly sampled within configurable bounds, and the floor, walls, and ceiling are assembled from modular tiles. Each wall segment has a configurable probability of being replaced by a door or window element, and the room is retextured with a random colour and pattern to introduce visual variety.

\subsubsection{Furniture Placement}

Furniture placement is divided into wall-mounted and free-standing categories. Wall-mounted items are positioned against available wall surfaces, while free-standing objects are placed using rejection sampling: candidate positions and orientations are accepted only if their \gls{OBB}s do not intersect previously placed objects. The total number of objects is controlled by a density parameter proportional to room area.

\subsection{Scan Management and Dataset Generation}
\label{sec:3_scan_management}

The virtual scanner and procedural room generator are coordinated by a \texttt{ScanManager} that orchestrates the automated generation of complete dataset entries. For each generated room, the scan manager executes the following sequence:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Room generation}: A new room is procedurally generated, and all detectable objects are identified within the scan volume.
    \item \textbf{Full scene scan}: The scene is scanned with objects present, producing the main point cloud and panoramic image. Ground-truth meshes and bounding boxes are exported for all detected objects.
    \item \textbf{Object isolation}: Each detected object's points are isolated from the full scan, producing partial point clouds per object. Optionally, per-object occlusion voxel grids are computed.
    \item \textbf{Empty scene scan}: All detected objects are deactivated, and the scene is scanned again to produce the ground-truth empty scene point cloud.
    \item \textbf{Scan variations}: Additional scans are generated with configurable variations in furniture layout, scanner type, and scanner position.
\end{enumerate}

Each variation is stored alongside the main scan with its acquisition parameters, enabling controlled experiments on the impact of individual factors. The variation system supports an arbitrary number of passes per room, each independently configurable.

\subsection{The V-Scan Dataset}
\label{sec:3_vscan}

Using the framework above, the V-Scan dataset was created as the main benchmark for scene and object completion in this thesis. The dataset combines properties not jointly available in existing resources: partial scene scans with complete geometric ground truth, multi-sensory outputs, temporal variations, and per-object annotations (Figure~\ref{fig:3_VScan}).

\subsubsection{Per-Scene Outputs}

For each scene folder, the following outputs are available:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Main scan}: coloured point cloud of the furnished scene.
    \item \textbf{Panoramic image}: equirectangular panoramic image from the scanner position.
    \item \textbf{Scan parameters}: JSON file recording the scanner configuration.
    \item \textbf{Room parameters}: JSON file recording the procedural generation parameters.
    \item \textbf{Object bounding boxes}: JSON file containing oriented bounding boxes for all detected objects.
    \item \textbf{Per-object data}: isolated partial point cloud, ground-truth complete mesh (PLY), and individual bounding box.
    \item \textbf{Empty scene scan}: coloured point cloud and panoramic image with all objects removed.
    \item \textbf{Scan variations}: additional point clouds and panoramic images for each configured variation.
\end{itemize}

This output structure enables evaluation at scene level (reconstructed empty scene vs.\ ground-truth empty scan), object level (completed objects vs.\ ground-truth meshes), and detection level (predicted bounding boxes vs.\ ground-truth annotations).

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:3_conclusion}

This chapter presented the data acquisition strategy adopted in this thesis through a combination of three complementary sources: publicly available datasets for training and qualitative evaluation, real-world multi-temporal campus captures for alignment evaluation, and the novel V-Scan synthetic dataset for quantitative evaluation of scene and object completion. Together, these sources address the evaluation of the full pipeline by providing the combination of partial scans, complete hidden geometry, multi-temporal variation, and multi-sensory captures.
