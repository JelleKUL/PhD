\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:5_intro}

This chapter prepares the aligned point cloud from Chapter~\ref{ch:4_Alignment_Updating} for the parallel scene and object completion stages through two complementary analyses: object isolation and occlusion detection (Figure~\ref{fig:5_intro}).

\begin{figure}[]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=1\textwidth]{Isolation_occlusion_intro}
  \caption[isolation and occlusion detection]{Overview of the object isolation and occlusion detection processes. The raw aligned point cloud (left) is processed to produce a set of isolated objects with refined bounding boxes (center) and a voxelised occlusion map  (right).}
  \label{fig:5_intro}
\end{figure}

Object isolation decomposes the scene into structural background elements and individual foreground objects. Since standard 3D detectors fit bounding boxes only to the visible geometry~\cite{qi_deep_2019}, partially scanned objects yield boxes that are too small or misoriented for the downstream completion methods, which require properly bounded and aligned partial observations~\cite{vermandere_guided_2025, mittal_autosdf_2022}. A refinement step is therefore applied that uses detected symmetry axes and physical constraints from adjacent scene planes to correct the bounding box orientation and extent prior to completion.

Occlusion detection then determines which regions of the scene were hidden from the scanner rather than genuinely empty, a distinction that is critical for guiding the completion stages without hallucinating geometry in observed free space. The analysis is performed at two levels simultaneously: a scene-level grid classifying the full room volume for the scene completion in Chapter~\ref{ch:6_Scene_Completion}, and per-object grids classifying the volume within each refined bounding box for the object completion in Chapter~\ref{ch:7_Object_Completion}.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:5_related}

This section reviews the four areas of prior work most relevant to the contributions of this chapter: 3D object detection in point clouds, symmetry detection in partial geometry, plane segmentation for structural element extraction, and occlusion detection and reasoning in scanned environments.


% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\subsection{3D Object Detection}
\label{sec:5_rw_detection}

Detecting objects directly in 3D point clouds has received considerable attention in recent years, driven by the availability of large-scale annotated indoor datasets such as ScanNet~\cite{dai_scannet_2017} and SUN RGB-D~\cite{song_sun_2015}. VoteNet~\cite{qi_deep_2019} introduced an end-to-end architecture that combines deep point set feature learning with a Hough voting mechanism: each input point generates a vote for potential object centres, and these votes are aggregated into clusters from which bounding box parameters are predicted. VoteNet demonstrated that purely geometric information, without relying on colour images, is sufficient for competitive 3D detection on real scans. Subsequent works such as MLCVNet~\cite{xie_mlcvnet_2020} and VoteNet+~\cite{ding_votenet_2020} refined this paradigm through multi-level contextual features and architectural improvements respectively. More recently, transformer-based approaches such as V-DETR~\cite{shen_v-detr_2023} and UniDet3D~\cite{kolodiazhnyi_unidet3d_nodate} have improved detection accuracy through better spatial attention mechanisms and cross-dataset generalisation, while AIC3DOD~\cite{cheng_aic3dod_nodate} incorporates room layout constraints as physical priors.

Despite these advances, all of these methods detect objects based on the geometry that is \textit{present} in the scan. They do not predict the spatial extent of the object beyond what is visible, which means the resulting bounding boxes are fitted to the partial observation rather than to the estimated complete object. This limitation necessitates a post-detection refinement step.


% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\subsection{Symmetry Detection}
\label{sec:5_rw_symmetry}
Symmetry detection in 3D shapes provides geometric cues that can be used to infer the orientation, centre and spatial extent of partially observed objects. Most manufactured indoor objects exhibit at least one axis of symmetry due to design and manufacturing constraints, making symmetry a useful prior for bounding box refinement.

For complete objects, PRS-Net~\cite{gao_prs-net_2019} employs an unsupervised 3D convolutional neural network to discover global planar reflective symmetry. However, complete geometry is rarely available for scanned objects. Partial symmetry detection is more challenging because there is no guarantee that symmetrical features will be captured. Mitra et al.~\cite{mitra_partial_2006} developed an early algorithm based on matching local shape signatures and accumulating evidence in transformation space, handling both partial and approximate symmetry. Sipiran et al.~\cite{sipiran_approximate_2014} improved upon this with a vote-based approach specifically targeting partial 3D meshes with large missing parts, using heat-diffusion-based surface functions and a partiality-aware voting scheme to reduce false positives. SymmetryNet~\cite{shi_symmetrynet_2020} requires only a single RGB-D input to discover symmetry planes using an unsupervised learning approach. Among these, the method by Sipiran et al. is most suited to the present setting because it operates directly on partial meshes without requiring training data or complete references, making it applicable to arbitrary scanned objects.


% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\subsection{Plane Segmentation}
\label{sec:5_rw_planes}

RANSAC-based plane detection remains the most widely used approach for extracting planar primitives from point clouds. Latent RANSAC~\cite{korman_latent_2018} accelerates hypothesis evaluation by learning to score RANSAC hypotheses in constant time, enabling efficient detection of dominant planar structures. Li et al.~\cite{li_improved_2017} proposed an improved RANSAC method based on Normal Distribution Transformation cells to avoid spurious planes in noisy data. Nurunnabi et al.~\cite{nurunnabi_robust_2016} introduced robust diagnostic principal component analysis for plane segmentation that handles outlier- and noise-contaminated data more effectively than standard RANSAC.

For indoor environments specifically, several works exploit the structural regularity of buildings. Jung et al.~\cite{jung_automated_2016} use constrained least-squares adjustment to produce wireframe models of indoor structures. Budroni and Boehm~\cite{budroni_automatic_2010} developed an automatic technique for reconstruction of Manhattan-world interior scenes using a volume sweep approach. Nikoohemat et al.~\cite{nikoohemat_exploiting_2017} combine plane detection with trajectory-based occlusion reasoning to detect openings and reconstruct walls, floors and ceilings in cluttered indoor environments captured by mobile laser scanners.


% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\subsection{Occlusion Detection}
\label{sec:5_rw_occlusion}
Chen et al.~\cite{chen_dynamic_2016} proposed a dynamic occlusion detection and inpainting method for sequential terrestrial laser scanning point clouds. Their approach constructs stationary background models using laser reflectance intensity and geometrical attributes across a TLS sequence, achieving high precision and recall but requiring multiple sequential scans from the same position.

Nikoohemat et al.~\cite{nikoohemat_change_2018} demonstrate the value of the scanner trajectory for occlusion reasoning in indoor mobile laser scanning. By using the trajectory as a set of scanner positions, gaps can be discriminated from real openings in the data, and a voxel-based method can be applied for labelling navigable space.

In the context of scene de-occlusion from images, Zhan et al.~\cite{zhan_self-supervised_2020} propose a self-supervised framework for recovering hidden scene structures. For object-level occlusion reasoning, Co-Section~\cite{strecke_where_2020} uses an object-level dynamic SLAM frontend to complete object shapes using hull and intersection constraints between objects and scene surfaces, which is conceptually related to the use of scene planes as intersection constraints in this chapter. Zhu et al.~\cite{zhu_occlusion_2020} address occlusion handling for industrial robots, demonstrating that occlusion reasoning is critical not only for reconstruction but also for planning and safety applications.

The approach proposed in this chapter differs from the above works in that it performs occlusion detection through voxelised ray marching from the known scanner position(s), operating at two levels simultaneously: scene-level occlusion for the entire room, and object-level occlusion within each detected oriented bounding box.

\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:5_methodology}

The methodology consists of two main components: object isolation (Section~\ref{sec:5_object_isolation}) and occlusion detection (Section~\ref{sec:5_occlusion_detection}). Object isolation begins by identifying and removal of the largest planar surfaces of the scene (floors, walls and ceilings) through iterative plane extraction, followed by object detection on the cleaned point cloud, symmetry detection on the isolated objects, and bounding box refinement using the detected symmetry axes and scene planes. Occlusion detection then determines which parts of the scene and of each individual object were hidden from the scanner. An overview of the full methodology is shown in Figure~\ref{fig:5_method_overview}.

\begin{figure}[]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=1\textwidth]{Detection_method}
  \caption[Object and occlusion detection pipeline]{Overview of the proposed object isolation and occlusion detection pipeline.}
  \label{fig:5_method_overview}
\end{figure}


% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\subsection{Object Isolation}
\label{sec:5_object_isolation}

The object isolation process consists of four sequential steps: plane-based structural segmentation of the scene, 3D object detection on the remaining foreground points, symmetry detection on the isolated partial objects, and bounding box refinement using the symmetry axes and structural planes.


% ── 5.3.1.1 ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\subsubsection{Plane-Based Structural Segmentation}
\label{sec:5_plane_detection}

The first step in object isolation is to separate the structural elements of the scene from the foreground objects. Indoor environments are predominantly composed of large planar surfaces such as walls, floors and ceilings, and these can be efficiently detected and removed before the object detection stage. Removing the structural geometry serves two purposes: it reduces the input complexity for the object detector by eliminating the dominant surfaces that are not objects of interest, and it produces the set of structural planes that will later be used to constrain the bounding box refinement (Figure~\ref{fig:5_plane_segmentation}).

\begin{figure}[h]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=1\textwidth]{Plane_segmentation}
  \caption[Plane-based structural segmentation]{The raw mesh (left) and the plane-segmented mesh (right) of a sample scene.}
  \label{fig:5_plane_segmentation}
\end{figure}

The structural segmentation is performed through a sequential iterative RANSAC plane detection~\cite{korman_latent_2018} on the full scene point cloud. The standard RANSAC algorithm randomly samples minimal point subsets (three points define a plane) to generate plane hypotheses and evaluates each hypothesis against the full point set by counting the number of inlier points within a distance threshold. The hypothesis with the largest number of inliers is accepted as a detected plane. In the iterative variant used here, this process is applied sequentially: at each iteration, the largest plane containing more than a predefined minimum number of inlier points is extracted and its inliers are removed from the remaining point set. The procedure is repeated until no planes with a sufficient number of inliers remain. After the procedure is complete, only a small number of residual points remain that do not fit any detected plane (Figure \ref{fig:5_plane_detection}).

\begin{figure}[h]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=1\textwidth]{scene_isolation}
  \caption[Plane-based structural segmentation]{Top: the original scene point cloud. Bottom: the detected surface planes (grey) and the remaining points after plane removal (coloured).}
  \label{fig:5_plane_detection}
\end{figure}

A simple semantic classification is then applied to each detected plane based on its normal direction. Planes with normals approximately aligned with the vertical axis are classified as floors or ceilings depending on their height, while planes with approximately horizontal normals are classified as walls. The points belonging to classified floor, wall and ceiling planes are removed from the scene, leaving predominantly foreground object geometry for the subsequent detection step.

To define proper physical boundaries for later use in bounding box refinement, the intersections between the detected planes are computed and used to define boundary quads. These boundaries, together with the plane normals, provide the geometric constraints that will limit the extent of the refined bounding boxes in Section~\ref{sec:5_bb_refinement}~\cite{vermandere_guided_2025}. The detected planes and their intersections are also reused in the scene completion stage (Chapter~\ref{ch:6_Scene_Completion}), where they form the basis for geometric reconstruction of the empty environment.


% ── 5.3.1.2 ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\subsubsection{3D Object Detection}
\label{sec:5_detection}

Object detection is performed on the cleaned point cloud, from which the structural planes have already been removed (Figure~\ref{fig:5_plane_detection}). A VoteNet-style detector~\cite{qi_deep_2019} is used, operating directly on the point cloud rather than on images. This is because the co-aligned and combined point clouds from the last chapter have fewer occlusions compared to image-only inputs. The scene is first sampled to a uniform resolution of 5\,cm, providing a suitable balance between geometric detail and execution speed~\cite{vermandere_guided_2025}.

\begin{figure}[h]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=1\textwidth]{Object_Detection_example}
  \caption[3D object detection example]{The remaining object point cloud(left) and the resulting detected bounding boxes highlighted around the detected objects(right).}
  \label{fig:5_object_detection}
\end{figure}


The VoteNet voting mechanism, described in Section~\ref{sec:5_rw_detection}, produces up-axis-aligned bounding box proposals for each detected object (Figure~\ref{fig:5_object_detection}). To mitigate over-detection, which is a common tendency in indoor scenes where furniture is closely spaced, overlapping bounding boxes are merged when their IoU exceeds 80\%~\cite{vermandere_guided_2025}. By performing the detection on the cleaned point cloud rather than the full scene, the detector can focus on the actual foreground objects without being distracted by the dominant structural surfaces that could otherwise generate spurious detections or absorb votes intended for nearby objects.

After detection, the objects are isolated from the scene. Rather than removing only the visible surface points, all points within the full bounding box volume are removed to ensure clean separation between object geometry and the surrounding environment. This process results in an incomplete scene with large rectangular holes that can be reconstructed in a later stage by the scene completion pipeline (Chapter~\ref{ch:6_Scene_Completion}).


% ── 5.3.1.3 ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\subsubsection{Symmetry Detection}
\label{sec:5_symmetry_detection}


The bounding boxes produced by the object detector are up-axis-aligned and fitted only to the visible points of each object. For proper completion, the bounding box must be oriented according to the object's principal axes and expanded to approximate the full spatial extent of the complete object, including its occluded portions. To exploit the symmetry typically present in manufactured indoor objects, the first step toward this refinement is detecting the symmetry axes of each partial object.

\begin{wrapfigure}[]{r}{0.25\textwidth}
  \centering
  \vspace{-10pt}
  \includegraphics[]{symmetry_example}
  \caption[Symmetry detection on partial object]{symmetry detection on partial object.}
  \label{fig:5_symmetry_detection}
\end{wrapfigure}
Approximate symmetry detection~\cite{sipiran_approximate_2014} is applied to the partial point cloud of each isolated object. As described in Section~\ref{sec:5_rw_symmetry}, the algorithm generates candidate symmetry planes through a vote-based matching of heat-diffusion surface descriptors. The detected symmetry planes provide two pieces of information that are essential for the subsequent bounding box refinement. First, the normal of the symmetry plane indicates one of the object's principal axes, which will be used as the ``right'' vector in the oriented bounding box construction. Second, the position of the symmetry plane provides an estimate of the object's geometric centre along that axis, enabling the bounding box to be centred symmetrically around the detected plane rather than being fitted only to the observed portion.

% ── 5.3.1.4 ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\subsubsection{Bounding Box Refinement}
\label{sec:5_bb_refinement}

The final step of the object isolation process combines the detected symmetry axis with the structural planes from the scene segmentation to construct a refined oriented bounding box for each object (Figure \ref{fig:5_bb_refinement_method}). The refinement proceeds in two stages: orientation estimation and boundary limiting.

\begin{figure}[ht]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=1\textwidth]{bb_refinement_method}
  \caption[Bounding box refinement]{The unaligned bounding box (left) is refined using the detected symmetry axis (center) and the adjacent scene planes to produce a properly oriented and bounded box (right).}
  \label{fig:5_bb_refinement_method}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{Orientation estimation.}
The oriented bounding box requires three mutually perpendicular axes. The first axis is obtained from the structural planes detected in Section~\ref{sec:5_plane_detection}: the adjacency of the object to each detected scene plane is computed to determine whether the object is grounded on the floor or mounted against a wall, and the normal $\mathbf{n}$ of the closest adjacent plane defines the first axis~\cite{vermandere_guided_2025}. The second axis is derived from the cross product of this plane normal with the primary symmetry axis $\mathbf{s}_1$ detected in Section~\ref{sec:5_symmetry_detection}:

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:5_axis2}
    \mathbf{a}_2 = \mathbf{n} \times \mathbf{s}_1
\end{equation}

When $\mathbf{n}$ and $\mathbf{s}_1$ are approximately parallel (i.e.\ $\|\mathbf{n} \times \mathbf{s}_1\| \approx 0$), the cross product degenerates and the secondary symmetry axis $\mathbf{s}_2$ is used instead. The third axis is then obtained as a final cross product to complete the orthogonal basis:

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:5_axis3}
    \mathbf{a}_3 = \mathbf{n} \times \mathbf{a}_2
\end{equation}

The resulting three perpendicular axes $(\mathbf{n},\, \mathbf{a}_2,\, \mathbf{a}_3)$ define the rotation used to orient the bounding box around the incomplete object.

\paragraph{Boundary limiting.}
Once the orientation is established, the scene planes are used to constrain the spatial extent of the bounding box. The bounding box boundaries are limited so that they do not extend through detected walls, floors or ceilings, preventing the bounding box from including regions that are physically impossible for the object to occupy. The symmetry axis is further used to centre the bounding box: when a clear symmetry plane is found, the bounding box is made symmetric about this plane, effectively predicting the spatial extent of the unobserved portion based on the observed portion. When an object exhibits multiple symmetry axes, the alignment can be further refined~\cite{vermandere_guided_2025}.


% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\subsection{Occlusion Detection}
\label{sec:5_occlusion_detection}


After object isolation and bounding box refinement, the next step is to determine which regions of the scene and of each individual object were occluded during scanning. This information is essential for the completion stages, which need to distinguish between regions that are confirmed empty and regions where geometry may be hidden behind visible surfaces.

\begin{wrapfigure}[12]{}{0.25\textwidth}
  \centering
  \vspace{-10pt}
  \includegraphics[width = 0.25\textwidth]{voxel_traversal}
  \caption[Voxelised ray marching]{Voxelised ray marching process. From \cite{ding_adaptive_2023}}
  \label{fig:5_voxel_traversal}
\end{wrapfigure}

The occlusion detection is performed through voxelised ray marching from the known scanner position(s) (Figure \ref{fig:5_voxel_traversal}). The approach operates at two levels: scene-level occlusion for the entire room, and object-level occlusion within each detected oriented bounding box.


% ── 5.3.2.1 ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\subsubsection{Voxelised Ray Marching}
\label{sec:5_raymarching}


The core operation is a discrete ray traversal through a voxel grid. Given the scanner position $\mathbf{o}$ and the set of scanned points $P = \{\mathbf{p}_i\}_{i=1}^{N}$, All voxels that contain one or more points $\mathbf{p}_i$ are marked as \textbf{occupied}. Then rays are traced from $\mathbf{o}$ through every unoccupied voxel in the voxel grid starting from the most outer voxels. The discrete traversal is performed using a 3D variant of the Bresenham line algorithm, which efficiently identifies all voxels intersected by each ray. For each ray, the \textbf{occupied} voxels and unmarked voxels along the path are classified into three categories:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Observed empty}: voxels that the ray passes through before passing an occupied voxel. These voxels are confirmed to contain no geometry.
    \item \textbf{Occupied}: voxels that contain scanned points. These voxels are confirmed to contain geometry.
    \item \textbf{Occluded}: voxels that lie behind an occupied voxel along the ray direction. These voxels could not be observed by the scanner and may or may not contain geometry.
\end{enumerate}

When multiple scanner positions are available, the voxel classification is accumulated across all positions: a voxel that is occluded from one scanner position but observed empty from another is classified as empty, since at least one observation confirms the absence of geometry.


% ── 5.3.2.2 ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\subsubsection{Scene-Level Occlusion}
\label{sec:5_scene_occlusion}

At the scene level, the voxel grid spans the entire room volume (Figure \ref{fig:5_scene_occlusion_example}). The ray marching is performed using all scanned points (before object removal) from all available scanner positions. This grid is used by the scene completion stage (Chapter~\ref{ch:6_Scene_Completion}) to constrain reconstruction to regions classified as occluded, preventing the generation of geometry in voxels that have been confirmed as empty by direct observation.

\begin{figure}[h]
  \centering
  \vspace{-10pt}
  \includegraphics[width=1\textwidth]{Scene_occlusion_example}
  \caption[Scene occlusion example]{The input pointcloud (left) and the calculated scene-level occlusion from the scanner position (right)}
  \label{fig:5_scene_occlusion_example}
  \vspace{-10pt}
\end{figure}

% ── 5.3.2.3 ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\subsubsection{Object-Level Occlusion}
\label{sec:5_object_occlusion}

\begin{wrapfigure}[]{}{0.4\textwidth}
  \centering
  \vspace{-10pt}
  \includegraphics[width = 0.4\textwidth]{Object_grid_example}
  \caption[Object-level voxel grid]{Object-level voxel grid example.}
  \label{fig:5_object_grid}
\end{wrapfigure}
At the object level, the voxel grid is defined within each object's refined \gls{OBB}. The use of oriented bounding boxes is important: because the bounding box is aligned with the object's principal axes rather than the world axes, the resulting voxel grid provides a more efficient and meaningful discretisation of the object's volume.

For each detected object, rays are traced from the scanner position(s) through the object-level voxel grid. The classification follows the same three-category scheme as the scene level, but is restricted to the volume enclosed by the oriented bounding box. The resulting per-object occlusion grid provides an initial estimate of which voxels need to be completed by the generative model in Chapter~\ref{ch:7_Object_Completion}. Additionally, when combined with the symmetry detection from the bounding box refinement step, the occluded voxels on one side of the symmetry plane can be initialised with mirrored values from the observed side, providing a geometric prior for the completion network~\cite{vermandere_guided_2025}.

\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:5_experiments}

The experimental evaluation is divided into three parts, corresponding to the three main components of the methodology: object detection, symmetry detection, and occlusion characterisation. All experiments are conducted on the V-Scan synthetic dataset (Section~\ref{sec:3_synthetic}), which provides both partial scans and complete ground-truth geometry including ground-truth bounding boxes. The symmetry detection is additionally evaluated on partial scans from the ShapeNet dataset~\cite{chang_shapenet_2015}. For qualitative analysis, objects from the Matterport3D dataset~\cite{chang_matterport3d_2017} are also used to demonstrate the applicability of the method to real-world scans.


% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\subsection{Object Detection}
\label{sec:5_exp_detection}

The object detection pipeline is evaluated on the V-Scan dataset across all five environment types (Figure~\ref{fig:5_detection_examples}). Since the V-Scan dataset provides ground-truth bounding boxes corresponding to the complete objects, the evaluation measures both detection accuracy and the quality of the spatial extent estimation.

\begin{figure}[ht]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=1\textwidth]{Object_detection}
  \caption[Object detection examples]{Results of the object detection pipeline on sample scenes from the V-Scan dataset. The ground-truth bounding boxes (left), the unrefined detection before scene-level processing (center), and the refined detection after scene-level processing (right) are shown for comparison.}
  \label{fig:5_detection_examples}
\end{figure}

\subsubsection{Evaluation Protocol}

The detection is evaluated using precision, recall and IoU between predicted and ground-truth bounding boxes. A predicted bounding box is considered a true positive when the IoU with a ground-truth box exceeds a threshold of 0.5, following the standard evaluation protocol defined in Chapter~\ref{ch:background}. Precision measures the fraction of predicted boxes that correspond to real objects, recall measures the fraction of ground-truth objects that are detected, and the average IoU quantifies the spatial accuracy of the matched detections.

\subsubsection{Analysis}

The overall precision of 0.495 indicates that roughly half of all predicted bounding boxes correspond to actual objects. The remaining false positives are partly expected, since the detector was originally trained on real-world datasets (ScanNet, SUN RGB-D) and applied here to synthetic V-Scan data, introducing a domain gap. Additionally, the over-detection tendency of VoteNet in densely furnished scenes, previously noted in~\cite{vermandere_guided_2025}, contributes to false positives despite the IoU-based merging of overlapping boxes. Precision is highest for bedrooms (0.612) and offices (0.598), where objects tend to be larger and more geometrically distinct, and lowest for living rooms (0.426) and industrial scenes (0.439), where smaller or more irregularly shaped objects lead to more spurious detections.

Recall varies across environment types. The bedroom and living room categories achieve the highest recall (0.870 and 0.794 respectively), which can be attributed to the presence of larger, more geometrically distinct objects such as beds, sofas and tables that are well represented in the detector's training data. The industrial environment shows the lowest recall (0.387), likely due to the presence of smaller or more irregularly shaped objects such as shelving units and equipment that differ substantially from the residential furniture the detector was trained on.

The average IoU across all scenes is 0.522, indicating moderate spatial overlap between predicted and ground-truth bounding boxes. Since the ground-truth boxes correspond to the complete objects while the predicted boxes are fitted to the visible geometry, the IoU is inherently limited by the degree of occlusion in each scene.

\subsection{Symmetry Detection}
\label{sec:5_exp_symmetry}

The symmetry detection component is evaluated independently on partial scans of single objects from the ShapeNet dataset~\cite{chang_shapenet_2015}. For each object, a partial scan is simulated by removing geometry beyond a randomly oriented clipping plane, mimicking the one-sided observations typical of indoor scanning.

\begin{figure}[ht]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=1\textwidth]{Symmetry_detection}
  \caption[Symmetry detection examples]{Examples of detected symmetry planes on partial ShapeNet models. Left: the partial point cloud. Centre: the detected primary symmetry plane. Right: comparison with the ground-truth symmetry plane.}
  \label{fig:5_symmetry_examples}
\end{figure}

For each model, up to three symmetry axes are detected and ranked by confidence score. The detected symmetry planes are compared against the ground-truth symmetry planes of the complete object. The evaluation metrics include the angular error between the detected and ground-truth symmetry plane normals, and the detection rate (the fraction of shapes for which the correct symmetry axis was found within a tolerance of $60^{\circ}$). Angular errors are averaged over detected axes only.

The primary symmetry axis, which is the most important for the bounding box refinement since it defines the ``right'' vector of the oriented bounding box, is detected reliably across all seven categories. Five of the seven categories (bookshelf, cabinet, lamp, sofa and table) achieve a 100\% primary detection rate, while chairs and beds reach 90.0\% and 75.0\% respectively. The mean angular error of the primary axis is below $4^{\circ}$ for all categories except lamps ($10.49^{\circ}$), where the often rotationally symmetric or irregular geometry of lampshades and bases makes it harder to distinguish a single dominant symmetry plane. The bed category shows the lowest primary detection rate (75.0\%), which can be attributed to asymmetric headboard and footboard configurations that break the bilateral symmetry assumed by the algorithm. Despite this, the angular error of $2.59^{\circ}$ for the beds where the axis is found indicates that when the symmetry is present, it is detected with high precision.

The secondary and tertiary axes show higher angular errors across all categories, typically between $6^{\circ}$ and $20^{\circ}$, and somewhat lower detection rates. This is expected for partial observations with average visibility fractions between 28.5\% and 37.8\%: many of the geometric features that define the secondary and tertiary symmetry planes are simply not present in the partial scan. Notably, highly regular objects such as cabinets and tables maintain strong detection rates (100\% and 100\%) and low errors even for their secondary axes ($6.06^{\circ}$ and $2.80^{\circ}$), reflecting the strong geometric regularity of these box-like or symmetric shapes. Chairs show the lowest secondary detection rate (75.0\%) with the highest secondary error among the evaluated categories ($13.75^{\circ}$), consistent with the diversity of chair designs and the fact that front-back symmetry is less robust to partial observation than left-right symmetry.

Overall, the results confirm that the symmetry detection is sufficiently reliable for the bounding box refinement across a representative range of indoor furniture categories. The primary axis, which is the one used for orientation estimation, is detected in at least 75\% of cases with angular errors typically well below $5^{\circ}$. Even where the primary detection rate is lower, the boundary limiting step using the scene planes can partially compensate for missing or inaccurate symmetry information.

\subsection{Bounding Box Refinement}
\label{sec:5_exp_refinement}

The bounding box refinement step is evaluated empirically on objects from the Matterport3D dataset~\cite{chang_matterport3d_2017} to assess the impact of the symmetry-guided orientation and plane-based boundary limiting under different spatial configurations. Figure~\ref{fig:5_bb_refinement_results} shows the results for a combination of free-standing objects with no adjacent walls and objects positioned against a wall.

\begin{figure}[ht]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=1\textwidth]{bb_refinement}
  \caption[Bounding box refinement results]{Bounding box refinement results on Matterport3D objects under three spatial configurations. Column 1: The captured object in the scene. Column 2: the detected mesh and its boundingbox. Column 3: the refined oriented bounding box after symmetry-guided orientation and plane-based boundary limiting.}
  \label{fig:5_bb_refinement_results}
\end{figure}

When the object is not positioned against a wall, the impact of the plane-based refinement is none, as the only relevant structural plane is the floor. Since the initial detection bounding boxes are aligned with the global axis and the floor is typically level, the bounding box aligns naturally with the floor plane. In this case, the refinement is driven primarily by the symmetry detection, which orients the bounding box according to the object's principal axis and centres it around the detected symmetry plane.

For objects positioned against a wall, the additional structural plane provides a clear physical boundary that constrains the bounding box extent. This is particularly valuable when the wall does not align with the global orthogonal axes, a situation that frequently occurs in non-rectangular rooms or rooms with angled walls. In such cases, the axis-aligned bounding box produced by the detector is misoriented with respect to the object, and the detected wall plane provides the correct alignment. For example, a cupboard positioned against a non-axis-aligned wall benefits from the detected wall plane, which provides a clear depth limit for the bounding box that the axis-aligned detection cannot capture~\cite{vermandere_guided_2025}.

The importance of proper alignment extends beyond the bounding box itself. As observed in~\cite{vermandere_guided_2025}, the orientation of the object within its bounding box has the greatest impact on the quality of the subsequent object completion results (Chapter~\ref{ch:7_Object_Completion}). When objects are not properly aligned, the completion network struggles to interpret the input and tends to produce distorted shapes. Even for objects with known boundaries, the network fails to complete the object meaningfully without proper orientation. This further underlines the importance of the symmetry-guided refinement for the overall pipeline, as misalignment at this stage propagates directly into degraded completion quality.

The occluded percentage shows substantial variation both across and within environment types. Scenes where chairs are clustered tightly around a central table create large shadow regions behind the furniture that are hidden from the scanner, while more open arrangements leave most of the room visible.

These statistics have direct implications for the downstream completion stages. Scenes with higher occlusion percentages present more challenging completion problems, as a larger fraction of the environment must be reconstructed from the occlusion prior alone.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:5_conclusion}

This chapter presented the object isolation and occlusion detection methodology that bridges the gap between the aligned point cloud produced in Chapter~\ref{ch:4_Alignment_Updating} and the completion stages presented in Chapters~\ref{ch:6_Scene_Completion} and~\ref{ch:7_Object_Completion}.

The object isolation pipeline begins with an iterative RANSAC-based plane segmentation that identifies and removes the structural elements of the scene, both to simplify the subsequent detection and to provide the physical boundary constraints needed for bounding box refinement. Object detection is then performed on the cleaned foreground point cloud using a VoteNet-based detector~\cite{qi_deep_2019}, achieving an overall precision of 0.495, recall of 0.551 and average IoU of 0.522 across 200 V-Scan scenes at an IoU threshold of 0.5. The detected bounding boxes are subsequently refined by combining approximate symmetry detection~\cite{sipiran_approximate_2014}, which identifies the primary axis with angular errors below $4^{\circ}$ and detection rates of at least 75\% across seven ShapeNet furniture categories, with the scene planes to construct oriented bounding boxes constrained by the physical boundaries of the room~\cite{vermandere_guided_2025, korman_latent_2018}.

The occlusion detection uses voxelised ray marching from the known scanner positions to classify the scene volume into observed empty, occupied and occluded regions at both scene level and object level. The occlusion characterisation on the V-Scan dataset showed that the fraction of occluded volume varies substantially across and within environment types, ranging from 7.97\% for office scenes to 22.07\% for dining rooms, with high intra-class variability reflecting the strong dependence on furniture arrangement and scanner placement.

Several limitations should be noted. The detection precision of 0.495 means that roughly half of the predicted boxes are false positives, and missed detections (overall recall of 0.551) propagate into all subsequent stages. The domain gap between the training data and the synthetic evaluation data likely contributes to this, and fine-tuning the detector or using more recent architectures could improve these results. The symmetry-based bounding box refinement assumes that objects exhibit at least one axis of symmetry, which holds for most manufactured indoor furniture but may not apply to irregular objects. While the primary axis is detected reliably across all tested categories, the secondary and tertiary axes show higher angular errors, limiting the refinement for objects that require multiple axes for proper alignment. Finally, the voxel resolution in the occlusion detection introduces a trade-off between spatial precision and computational cost whose optimal setting depends on the scale and complexity of the scene.

Despite these limitations, the outputs of this chapter provide the structured inputs required for the parallel completion stages. The scene-level occlusion grid informs Chapter~\ref{ch:6_Scene_Completion} about where environmental reconstruction is needed, while the per-object occlusion grids and refined bounding boxes provide Chapter~\ref{ch:7_Object_Completion} with the spatial context and geometric priors necessary for plausible object reconstruction.
