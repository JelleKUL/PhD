\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:6_intro}

% Paragraph purpose: situate the chapter in the pipeline.
This chapter addresses the environment completion stage of the pipeline, reconstructing the permanent structural shell of the room from the object-removed point cloud, the structural planes and the scene-level occlusion grid produced in Chapter~\ref{ch:5_Scene_Segmentation}, together with the panoramic image acquired during the scan. The output is a closed textured mesh of the empty room (Figure~\ref{fig:6_intro}).

\begin{figure}[hbht]
 \centering
 \includegraphics[width=1\textwidth]{Scene_completion_intro}
 \caption[Scene completion introduction]{The object-removed point cloud (left) is converted into a closed textured mesh of the empty environment (right).}
 \label{fig:6_intro}
\end{figure}

% Paragraph purpose: frame the technical problem.
Two sources of missing geometry must be reconciled. Object removal cuts foreground furniture out along its bounding box, leaving large rectangular holes where the background was occluded; scanner occlusion leaves additional holes wherever the line of sight was blocked. The geometry behind both must be inferred, but only where the scene-level occlusion grid confirms that the surface was hidden rather than genuinely absent. Reconstructing per detected plane, rather than over the unstructured point cloud, keeps the inferred geometry physically plausible and avoids the bulging or non-manifold artefacts that scene-level volumetric methods produce on the large planar surfaces that dominate indoor environments~\cite{hubner_voxel-based_2020, dai_spsg_2020, roldao_3d_2022}.

% Paragraph purpose: state the two-stage approach and its scope.
The completion is split into a geometric stage that turns each detected plane into a watertight mesh fragment following the scanned surface where it was observed and spanning the holes where it was not, and a textural stage that maps the panorama onto each fragment, masks the pixels belonging to removed or occluded regions, and inpaints them~\cite{vermandere_semantic_2024, suvorov_resolution-robust_2022}. The rooms are reconstructed as solid closed walls; openings such as windows and doors are deferred to the dynamification stage in Chapter~\ref{ch:8_Scene_Dynamification}, where they are reintroduced as interactive elements.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:6_related}

This section reviews the three areas of prior work most relevant to the contributions of this chapter: geometric reconstruction of surfaces from point clouds, image inpainting, and combined object removal with scene inpainting.


% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\subsection{Geometry Reconstruction}
\label{sec:6_rw_geometry}

Reconstructing a continuous surface from an unstructured point cloud is a long-standing problem in geometry processing. Screened Poisson surface reconstruction~\cite{kazhdan_screened_2013} produces watertight surfaces from oriented point sets by solving a screened Poisson equation that interpolates the input points, and Marching Cubes~\cite{lorensen_marching_1998} extracts the surface from a discretised field. These general-purpose methods tend to close large gaps with smooth bulging surfaces and frequently produce holes or non-manifold geometry that require further repair~\cite{attene_lightweight_2010}, which is undesirable for the flat structural elements of a room where the missing regions should remain planar.

For indoor environments specifically, the structural regularity of buildings is commonly exploited by reconstructing walls, floors and ceilings from detected planar primitives rather than from the raw points. Budroni and Boehm~\cite{budroni_automatic_2010} reconstruct Manhattan-world interiors through a volume sweep over detected planes, and Jung et al.~\cite{jung_automated_2016} use constrained least-squares adjustment to produce wireframe models of indoor structures. Hübner et al.~\cite{hubner_voxel-based_2020} voxelise the input geometry and apply a rule-based sweep to label walls, wall openings and empty interior without assuming a Manhattan-world layout or perfectly planar surfaces. Li et al.~\cite{li_hybrid_2024} segment the room point cloud into external planar structure and internal objects, reconstructing the structure as a lightweight polygonal model from intersecting faces while replacing the objects with fitted CAD models. Nikoohemat et al.~\cite{nikoohemat_exploiting_2017, nikoohemat_change_2018} combine plane detection with trajectory-based occlusion reasoning to recover the structural surfaces of cluttered interiors. These plane-based approaches motivate the per-plane reconstruction strategy used in this chapter, which keeps the reconstructed structure flat while still following the scanned surface where it was observed.


% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\subsection{Image Inpainting}
\label{sec:6_rw_inpainting}

Image inpainting fills missing or masked regions of an image with plausible content. Classical methods propagate surrounding colour and structure into the hole~\cite{galerne_texture_2017, maggiordomo_texture_2023}, but they struggle with the large masks that result from removing entire pieces of furniture. Learning-based methods have largely replaced them. LaMa~\cite{suvorov_resolution-robust_2022} uses fast Fourier convolutions to provide a large receptive field, which makes it particularly effective at completing wide masks and repetitive structures such as walls and floors while generalising to resolutions higher than those seen during training. Diffusion-based approaches such as Stable Diffusion~\cite{rombach_high-resolution_2021} and the Inpaint Anything pipeline~\cite{yu_inpaint_2023} produce highly detailed results but can introduce content that is inconsistent with the surrounding surface and are computationally heavier. For the planar surfaces reconstructed in this chapter, where the goal is a continuous texture rather than novel detail, the large-mask robustness of LaMa is the most suitable choice.


% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\subsection{Object Removal and Scene Inpainting}
\label{sec:6_rw_defurnishing}

A growing body of work addresses the combined problem of removing furniture from a captured scene and filling in the revealed background, often referred to as defurnishing. Semantic scene completion methods such as SPSG~\cite{dai_spsg_2020} and broader surveys of the field~\cite{roldao_3d_2022} jointly infer missing geometry and colour but treat the scene as a monolithic volume and do not separate the structural background from the removed clutter. Wei et al.~\cite{wei_clutter_2023} detect and remove clutter from 3D scenes and inpaint the resulting holes with view-consistent geometry and texture by merging individually inpainted RGB-D images. For panoramic input specifically, Slavcheva et al.~\cite{slavcheva_empty_2024} defurnish indoor panoramas using a fine-tuned Stable Diffusion model with increased context and improved blending, producing geometrically plausible inpaints without explicit room layout estimation.

NeRFiller~\cite{weber_nerfiller_2023} completes missing portions of a 3D capture through generative 3D inpainting but focuses on completing scenes rather than removing foreground objects and operates on neural radiance fields rather than explicit meshes. Closest to the present setting is the defurnishing pipeline of Dolhasz et al.~\cite{dolhasz_defurnishing_2025}, which jointly removes furniture from both a textured mesh and its panoramic images: it segments and removes furniture from the mesh, extends planes and fills holes to obtain a simplified structural mesh, and uses geometric edges rendered from that mesh to guide a ControlNet inpainting of the panoramas. These methods inpaint on the panoramic views, which couples the texture result to the particular viewpoint and can leave inconsistencies between views. The method proposed in this chapter instead inpaints each reconstructed plane individually in its own flattened texture space~\cite{vermandere_semantic_2024}, decoupling the result from the viewpoint and keeping the inpainting layout geometrically consistent with the planar surface.

\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:6_methodology}

The scene completion methodology proceeds in two stages (Figure~\ref{fig:6_method_overview}). The geometric stage (Section~\ref{sec:6_geometry}) turns each detected plane into a watertight mesh fragment, and the textural stage (Section~\ref{sec:6_texture}) projects, masks and inpaints the panorama onto these fragments.

\begin{figure}[hbht]
 \centering
 \includegraphics[width=1\textwidth]{Scene_completion_method}
 \caption[Scene completion pipeline]{Overview of the scene completion pipeline, from the object-removed point cloud to the final textured mesh.}
 \label{fig:6_method_overview}
\end{figure}


% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\subsection{Geometry Reconstruction}
\label{sec:6_geometry}

The geometric stage consists of three steps: plane instance segmentation, surface point generation, and surface projection.


% ── 6.3.1.1 ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\subsubsection{Plane Instance Segmentation}
\label{sec:6_plane_segmentation}

The plane set $\{\Pi_k\}$ produced by the iterative RANSAC step of Chapter~\ref{ch:5_Scene_Segmentation}, re-run on the object-removed point cloud~\cite{korman_latent_2018}, provides one implicit model per dominant structural surface together with its supporting points. Each $\Pi_k$ is infinite and overlaps with the others, so it must be cut into bounded, non-overlapping fragments before it can be meshed (Figure~\ref{fig:6_plane_segmentation}).

\begin{figure}[hbht]
 \centering
 \includegraphics[width=1\textwidth]{Scene_completion_plane_segmentation}
 \caption[Plane instance segmentation]{The detected planes (left) are cut into bounded fragments by their mutual intersections and filtered to retain only the fragments supported by the scan (right).}
 \label{fig:6_plane_segmentation}
\end{figure}

For each $\Pi_k$ an oversized quad is initialised at the size of the scene bounding box and then clipped against every other detected plane $\Pi_l$ ($l \neq k$) using the Sutherland-Hodgman algorithm~\cite{sutherland_reentrant_1974}. Each split divides the current fragment into the parts in front of and behind the cutting plane; both are retained with their normals $\vec{\mathbf{n}}_k$ oriented toward the scene centre. After cutting against all other planes, each plane is represented by a set of fragments whose edges coincide with plane-plane intersections, giving tight seams where walls meet the floor and ceiling.

Many fragments fall in regions where the corresponding surface was never scanned and therefore do not represent real geometry. Each fragment is tested against the segmented plane points by projecting nearby points onto the fragment and counting those that lie within one voxel of its surface and inside its 2D boundary. A fragment is kept if its inlier count exceeds a fixed threshold (1000 points in the experiments) and discarded otherwise, leaving only the fragments that correspond to observed structural surfaces.


% ── 6.3.1.2 ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\subsubsection{Surface Point Generation}
\label{sec:6_point_generation}

The retained fragments are flat and pass through the scanned surface, but the target mesh must follow the real surface where it was observed and span the holes left by object removal and occlusion. To enable this, each fragment is first densified to a uniform sampling at the scene voxel size, matching the density of the scanned point cloud (Figure~\ref{fig:6_point_sampling}).

\begin{figure}[hbht]
 \centering
 \includegraphics[width=1\textwidth]{Scene_completion_point_sampling}
 \caption[Point sampling for scene completion]{Synthetic points (red) are generated on each fragment to fill the gaps in the scanned point cloud (coloured), producing an evenly sampled surface.}
 \label{fig:6_point_sampling}
\end{figure}

For each fragment, points are sampled uniformly on its surface and voxel-downsampled to the scene voxel size. Only samples lying further than one voxel from any existing scanned point are retained, so that synthetic points fill the unoccupied gaps without duplicating observed geometry. The retained samples are combined with the original segmented points of that plane to form a complete, evenly sampled point set $P_k$ for the surface.


% ── 6.3.1.3 ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\subsubsection{Surface Projection}
\label{sec:6_surface_projection}

Each plane fragment is remeshed into a regular grid at the voxel density. The interior is filled with grid points spaced one voxel apart, and the boundary edges produced by the plane cutting are subdivided at the same spacing and preserved exactly so that adjacent fragments keep their shared seams. The interior and boundary vertices are then triangulated together using a constrained Delaunay triangulation~\cite{delaunay_sur_1934} that respects the boundary segments, yielding a mesh $M_k = (V_k, E_k, F_k)$ per fragment (Figure~\ref{fig:6_mesh_projection}).

\begin{figure}[hbht]
 \centering
 \includegraphics[width=1\textwidth]{Scene_completion_mesh_projection}
 \caption[Mesh projection for scene completion]{The subdivided mesh (left) is projected onto the combined point set $P_k$, producing a mesh that follows the scanned surface where it was observed (right).}
 \label{fig:6_mesh_projection}
\end{figure}

Each interior vertex $\mathbf{v}_i \in V_k$ is displaced along the plane normal $\vec{\mathbf{n}}_k$ to the nearest point of $P_k$, found through a k-d tree query, so that the reconstructed surface follows the small undulations present in the real scan rather than remaining perfectly flat. The boundary vertices are kept in place to maintain a tight fit against the neighbouring fragments. Triangle normals are finally oriented toward the recorded scanner position so that the resulting surface faces into the room. The union $\bigcup_k M_k$ forms the closed structural shell of the empty room, conforming to the scanned surfaces where they were observed and spanning the regions that were occluded or removed.


% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\subsection{Texture Reconstruction}
\label{sec:6_texture}

With the geometry reconstructed, the second stage assigns a continuous texture to each plane through a per-plane UV parameterisation, a panorama projection step, an occlusion-mask transfer, and a per-plane inpainting step.


% ── 6.3.2.1 ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\subsubsection{UV Parameterisation}
\label{sec:6_uv}

Each fragment $M_k$ is mapped to a 2D \gls{UV} space so that it can be textured and inpainted as a regular image~\cite{vermandere_semantic_2024} (Figure~\ref{fig:6_uv_parameterisation}). The parameterisation is built from the fragment normal $\vec{\mathbf{n}}_k$ and a consistent up direction $\vec{\mathbf{u}}_k$: the world vertical axis for vertical surfaces, and a fixed horizontal axis for floors and ceilings. The horizontal texture axis is $\vec{\mathbf{u}}_k \times \vec{\mathbf{n}}_k$, the vertical axis completes the right-handed frame, and the vertices are projected onto this frame and normalised to the unit square $\mathbf{c}_i = (u_i, v_i) \in [0,1]^2$.

\begin{figure}[hbht]
 \centering
 \includegraphics[width=1\textwidth]{Scene_completion_uv_map}
 \caption[UV parameterisation for scene completion]{A reference UV checker texture (left) is mapped to each fragment individually to visualise the per-plane parameterisation (right).}
 \label{fig:6_uv_parameterisation}
\end{figure}

Keeping the up direction and the orientation toward the scanner consistent across fragments ensures that inpainting operates on an upright, logically laid out image. This consistency has been shown to improve the plausibility of the inpainted result, since the network sees walls and floors in the same canonical orientation as the data it was trained on~\cite{vermandere_semantic_2024}.


% ── 6.3.2.2 ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\subsubsection{Panorama Projection and Occlusion Masking}
\label{sec:6_pano_projection}

For each fragment, the panorama is sampled to produce its source texture: a regular grid of points is laid out in the UV frame, lifted back into 3D, and projected into the equirectangular panorama through the inverse of the scanner transform to retrieve the corresponding colour. This yields one texture image per fragment, aligned with its UV parameterisation.

The panorama still contains all foreground objects, so the pixels covering removed or occluded geometry must be masked before they are baked into the surface. The mask is derived from the scene-level occlusion grid $\mathbf{G}$ computed in Chapter~\ref{ch:5_Scene_Segmentation} (Figure~\ref{fig:6_pano_occlusion}). For each pixel of the panorama, the corresponding ray is tested against $\mathbf{G}$ and the reconstructed surface: pixels whose ray hits an occluded voxel $g_{ijk}$ before reaching the reconstructed surface are flagged. Equivalently, comparing the depth of the reconstructed planes against the captured scene depth marks the pixels that lie closer than the reconstructed surface. The flagged pixels are transferred onto each fragment's projected texture, producing one mask per plane (Figure~\ref{fig:6_to_be_inpainted}).

\begin{figure}[hbht]
 \centering
 \includegraphics[width=1\textwidth]{Scene_completion_pano_occlusion}
 \caption[Panoramic projection and occlusion masking]{The occluded voxel grid (bottom left) is projected onto the panorama (right) to identify the pixels belonging to removed or occluded geometry. The masked pixels are highlighted in red (bottom right) and used as the inpainting mask.}
 \label{fig:6_pano_occlusion}
\end{figure}

\begin{figure}[hbht]
 \centering
 \includegraphics[width=1\textwidth]{Scene_completion_to_be_inpainted}
 \caption[Projected mask on reconstructed mesh]{The occluded pixels (red) projected onto the reconstructed mesh, showing the regions that will be inpainted per plane.}
 \label{fig:6_to_be_inpainted}
\end{figure}


% ── 6.3.2.3 ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\subsubsection{Texture Inpainting}
\label{sec:6_inpainting}

Each masked plane texture is inpainted individually. The mask is first dilated by a fixed margin (20 pixels in the experiments) to remove any fringe of clutter or movable furniture that may remain around the edges of the masked regions. The dilated mask and the masked texture are then passed to LaMa~\cite{suvorov_resolution-robust_2022}, which fills the missing region with content consistent with the surrounding wall or floor texture (Figure~\ref{fig:6_plane_inpainting}). Inpainting per plane rather than on the panorama directly means the network operates on a flat, upright surface with a coherent layout, which suits the repetitive planar structures it is asked to complete and keeps the result independent of the original viewpoint.

\begin{figure}[hbht]
 \centering
 \includegraphics[width=1\textwidth]{Scene_completion_plane_inpainting}
 \caption[Plane-based inpainting]{The masked plane textures (left) are inpainted by LaMa~\cite{suvorov_resolution-robust_2022}, producing completions consistent with the surrounding surface (right).}
 \label{fig:6_plane_inpainting}
\end{figure}

The inpainted textures are applied back to their UV-parameterised fragments, and the textured fragments are combined into the final mesh of the completed environment (Figure~\ref{fig:6_final_completion}). This empty scene, together with the isolated objects from Chapter~\ref{ch:5_Scene_Segmentation}, forms the input to the object completion stage in Chapter~\ref{ch:7_Object_Completion} and the dynamification stage in Chapter~\ref{ch:8_Scene_Dynamification}.

\begin{figure}[hbht]
 \centering
 \includegraphics[width=1\textwidth]{Scene_completion_result}
 \caption[Final scene completion]{The final completed empty scene with inpainted textures applied to the reconstructed mesh.}
 \label{fig:6_final_completion}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Evaluation Protocol}
\label{sec:6_exp_protocol}

Geometric accuracy is measured against the ground-truth empty geometry using the Chamfer distance and the F-score at a 5\,cm threshold defined in Chapter~\ref{ch:background}, both computed between points sampled on the reconstructed and ground-truth surfaces. The Chamfer distance reports the average bidirectional surface distance in metres and the F-score at 5\,cm reports the fraction of reconstructed and ground-truth points within 5\,cm of each other.

Textural accuracy is measured by comparing the inpainted plane textures against the ground-truth empty panorama reprojected onto the same planes, using the three image quality metrics defined in Chapter~\ref{ch:background}: \gls{PSNR}, \gls{SSIM} and \gls{LPIPS}~\cite{zhang_unreasonable_2018}. PSNR and SSIM quantify pixel-level and structural fidelity (higher is better), while LPIPS measures perceptual similarity in a deep feature space (lower is better). Reprojecting the ground-truth panorama onto the reconstructed planes ensures that the same UV parameterisation is used for both predictions and references, so the comparison is independent of UV layout differences.


% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\subsection{Results on V-Scan}
\label{sec:6_exp_vscan}

\paragraph{Geometric accuracy.}
The geometric reconstruction is consistently accurate across all environment types, with Chamfer distances between 1.5\,cm and 1.9\,cm and F-scores at 5\,cm above 0.975. Almost all of the reconstructed surface therefore lies within one voxel of the ground truth. The residential scenes (bedroom, dining room, living room) have slightly lower Chamfer distances than the industrial and office scenes, reflecting their simpler and more regular planar layouts. The industrial and office categories are the most challenging geometrically: their larger extents and more complex structural surfaces push more reconstruction into the occluded regions, which are filled by synthetic point generation rather than constrained by the scan. The tight standard deviations (below 2.2\,mm Chamfer in all classes) confirm that the per-plane reconstruction behaves robustly regardless of furniture arrangement, because the geometry is constrained to the detected structural planes rather than inferred over the unstructured points.

\paragraph{Textural accuracy.}
The textural results show a clearer separation between scene types. The residential scenes achieve high fidelity, with the bedroom reaching a PSNR of 59.25\,dB, an SSIM of 0.9902 and an LPIPS of 0.0121, because their walls and floors are largely uniform and the masked regions are surrounded by repetitive texture that LaMa~\cite{suvorov_resolution-robust_2022} completes reliably. The dining room and living room sit between these and the industrial cases (51.25\,dB and 48.30\,dB respectively). The industrial and office scenes score noticeably lower (PSNR around 43--45\,dB, LPIPS around 0.045--0.05), because they contain larger occluded regions and more textured, less repetitive surfaces where the inpainted content diverges more from the ground truth.

\paragraph{Link to occlusion characterisation.}
This pattern mirrors the occlusion characterisation in Chapter~\ref{ch:5_Scene_Segmentation}: the industrial scenes showed the largest absolute occluded volumes and the dining rooms the highest variability, while the bedrooms and offices remained small. The textural results follow the same ordering up to a swap between offices and dining rooms, confirming that textural difficulty is driven primarily by the amount of surface that must be reconstructed from the inpainting prior rather than observed directly, and secondarily by the visual complexity of that surface. The high standard deviation of PSNR within residential classes (11--12\,dB) reflects this: scenes with little occlusion behind furniture reach the upper end of the range, while scenes with heavily obscured walls drop closer to the industrial regime.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:6_conclusion}
 
This chapter presented a per-plane scene completion methodology that turns the object-removed point cloud, the structural planes and the scene-level occlusion grid from Chapter~\ref{ch:5_Scene_Segmentation} into a closed textured mesh of the empty room, combining a constrained per-plane geometric reconstruction with a per-plane LaMa~\cite{suvorov_resolution-robust_2022} inpainting in flattened UV space~\cite{vermandere_semantic_2024}.
 
On V-Scan, the geometry is reconstructed within a Chamfer distance of 1.5--1.9\,cm and an F-score above 0.975 across all environment types, with the tightest values on residential scenes and the largest on industrial and office scenes. Texturally, the same ordering holds: residential rooms reach up to 59.25\,dB PSNR and 0.012 LPIPS, while industrial and office scenes drop to 43--45\,dB and 0.045--0.050 LPIPS, confirming that textural difficulty is driven primarily by the amount of surface that has to be filled from the inpainting prior rather than by the geometric reconstruction itself.
 
The main limitations are the planarity assumption, which excludes curved structural surfaces, and the dependence on a single aligned panorama, which limits textural recovery when occluded regions are large or highly textured. The closed-wall assumption is intentional: openings such as windows and doors are reintroduced as interactive elements in Chapter~\ref{ch:8_Scene_Dynamification}.
 
Together with the isolated objects from Chapter~\ref{ch:5_Scene_Segmentation}, the completed environment forms the input to Chapter~\ref{ch:7_Object_Completion}, where the reconstructed planes also act as the physical boundary constraints that resolve the mutual dependency between scene-level and object-level completion identified in Chapter~\ref{ch:introduction}~\cite{vermandere_guided_2025}.
