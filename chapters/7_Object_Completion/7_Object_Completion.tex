\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:7_intro}

% Paragraph purpose: situate the chapter in the pipeline and state the problem.
This chapter addresses the object completion stage of the pipeline. Each isolated foreground object delivered by Chapter~\ref{ch:5_Scene_Segmentation} must be turned into an independent, complete and textured mesh. Two outputs are needed simultaneously: a complete geometry that covers the surface the scanner could not observe, and a complete texture that covers the corresponding appearance. Both are unknown beyond the visible portion of the object, and unlike the planar walls and floors of Chapter~\ref{ch:6_Scene_Completion}, indoor furniture and equipment have arbitrary topology, free-form curvature and category-specific detail, so the per-plane reconstruction strategy used for the environment does not apply. A learned model is therefore needed to infer the missing parts.

% Paragraph purpose: introduce the two methods as a response to this problem.
Two complementary methodologies are proposed in response, summarised at a glance in Table~\ref{tab:7_method_comparison} and outlined in Figure~\ref{fig:7_intro}. The first relies on a voxelised distance field of the partial object together with the per-object occlusion grid of Chapter~\ref{ch:5_Scene_Segmentation}, and uses AutoSDF~\cite{mittal_autosdf_2022} to complete the missing voxels (Section~\ref{sec:7_voxel}). The second uses a cropped image of the object together with its partial point cloud, and uses Hunyuan3D-Omni~\cite{hunyuan3d_hunyuan3d-omni_2025} to generate the complete shape from the image (Section~\ref{sec:7_image}). The two methods sit on opposite ends of the input spectrum: one consumes a geometry-only voxel representation, the other an image-led representation with geometric guidance. Together they cover the range of failure modes that arise when one or the other input source is sparse, noisy or out of distribution.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:7_related}

This section reviews the three areas most relevant to the contributions of this chapter: 3D object completion networks, texture completion for completed objects and Material detection.


% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\subsection{3D Object Completion Networks}
\label{sec:7_rw_completion}

% Paragraph purpose: retrieval-based first (oldest paradigm, weakest assumption)
Early approaches to object completion do not predict the missing geometry, but \textit{replace} the partial scan with the closest match from a CAD database. RetrievalFuse~\cite{siddiqui_retrievalfuse_2021} learns embeddings of both scene chunks and database items so that the most similar prior can be assembled around a partial scan. Earlier hybrid pipelines~\cite{li_hybrid_2024} segment the room point cloud into structural and object components, reconstruct the structural elements from detected planes, and replace the detected objects with fitted CAD models. Database-driven completion produces clean, watertight meshes for free, but its assumption is also its weakness: any object without a close neighbour in the database is replaced by a wrong category, and the heterogeneous furniture and equipment encountered in real indoor scans are exactly where such mismatches occur. For this reason, retrieval-based methods are not considered further in the rest of this chapter.

% Paragraph purpose: point-based methods
Point-based methods operate directly on a fixed-size point cloud. Point-Voxel Diffusion~\cite{zhou_3d_2021} predicts the complete shape through a 3D diffusion process, and IF-Net~\cite{chibane_implicit_2020} uses implicit features extracted from the partial point cloud to predict occupancy at arbitrary query positions. These models recover the broad shape reliably but lose fine detail in dense regions and struggle with large missing parts, because the point representation does not encode a continuous surface and the partial sampling drops fine features along with the missing geometry.

% Paragraph purpose: voxelised distance-field methods + AutoSDF figure
Voxelised distance-field methods convert the partial geometry to an SDF or, for non-watertight scans, a UDF, both defined in Chapter~\ref{ch:background}, and complete the field on a regular voxel grid. AutoSDF~\cite{mittal_autosdf_2022} learns a discrete autoregressive prior over voxelised SDF grids using a \gls{VQ-VAE} and predicts the missing voxels conditioned on the observed ones (Figure~\ref{fig:7_autosdf}). SDFusion~\cite{cheng_sdfusion_2023} extends this with multimodal conditioning such as text or images, and DiffComplete~\cite{chu_diffcomplete_2023} reformulates the problem as a diffusion process. PatchComplete~\cite{rao_patchcomplete_2022} and ShapeFormer~\cite{yan_shapeformer_2023} use multi-resolution patch priors and a sparse vector-quantised implicit representation respectively to improve generalisation to unseen categories. XCube~\cite{ren_xcube_2023} scales the same idea to large scenes through a hierarchical sparse voxel latent diffusion model. The Interactive Voxel Editor of~\cite{wegen_interactive_2022} adds a complementary contribution: it shows that the voxel-level mask of the to-be-completed region can be edited by hand in a 3D interface, which is the workflow followed in~\cite{vermandere_guided_2025}.

\begin{figure}[hbht]
 \centering
 \includegraphics[width=1\textwidth]{Auto_SDF}
 \caption[AutoSDF method overview]{AutoSDF~\cite{mittal_autosdf_2022}: the partial SDF is encoded into discrete latent tokens by a \gls{VQ-VAE}; a transformer then autoregressively predicts the latent tokens for the voxels marked as to-be-completed, conditioned on the observed ones, and the result is decoded back to a complete SDF.}
 \label{fig:7_autosdf}
\end{figure}

% Paragraph purpose: image-based methods (no Hunyuan2.0 mention here) + figure
Image-based generative methods bypass the voxelisation step and predict 3D geometry directly from one or more images. SAM3D~\cite{team_sam_2025} combines segmentation masks with a large-scale latent flow transformer to estimate coarse geometry, pose and texture, decoded into meshes and Gaussian splats. Trellis~\cite{xiang_structured_2025} introduces a structured latent representation that can be decoded into multiple 3D formats through rectified flow transformers. Hunyuan3D-Omni~\cite{hunyuan3d_hunyuan3d-omni_2025} proposes a unified cross-modal framework that accepts an image together with optional 3D priors such as point clouds, bounding boxes or skeletons, enabling controllable generation under partial input (Figure~\ref{fig:7_hunyuan3d}).

\begin{figure}[hbht]
 \centering
 \includegraphics[width=1\textwidth]{Hunyuan_3D}
 \caption[Hunyuan3D-Omni method overview]{Hunyuan3D-Omni~\cite{hunyuan3d_hunyuan3d-omni_2025}: a DINO-based image encoder and a point encoder produce condition features that are concatenated and fed to a Diffusion Transformer; the decoded latent is converted to an SDF and meshed.}
 \label{fig:7_hunyuan3d}
\end{figure}

% Paragraph purpose: chosen methods in their own closing paragraph
Two of the above are selected for this chapter. AutoSDF is selected for the voxel-based pipeline because it explicitly supports voxel-level guidance over the to-be-completed region, which is exactly the signal that the per-object occlusion grid of Chapter~\ref{ch:5_Scene_Segmentation} provides automatically. Hunyuan3D-Omni is selected for the image-based pipeline because it accepts the partial point cloud as an additional geometric control input, which keeps the generated shape anchored to the observed surface rather than letting it drift to whatever the image alone suggests.


% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\subsection{Texture Completion}
\label{sec:7_rw_texture}

% Paragraph purpose: pure 2D inpainting methods that need a UV map
Once the geometry is complete, the missing texture can be recovered by treating the object's texture map as an image and inpainting the unobserved regions. Classical 2D approaches model textures as Gaussian processes and perform conditional sampling for inpainting~\cite{galerne_texture_2017}, and patch-based image quilting~\cite{efros_image_2001} synthesises new texture by stitching together blocks from a reference region. Learning-based methods such as MAT~\cite{li_mat_2022} and CM-GAN~\cite{zheng_cm-gan_2022} employ transformer architectures and cascaded modulation to handle large missing regions, while Inpaint Anything~\cite{yu_inpaint_2023} combines segmentation models with diffusion-based inpainters. LaMa~\cite{suvorov_resolution-robust_2022}, used for scene completion in Chapter~\ref{ch:6_Scene_Completion}, is particularly effective at completing wide masks on repetitive structures but is less suited to the detailed and category-specific textures of foreground objects.

% Paragraph purpose: 2D inpainting needs a good UV map
A common assumption of all of these methods is that adjacent regions in the texture image correspond to adjacent regions on the surface, since the inpainting decisions are driven entirely by the local pixel neighbourhood. This assumption breaks for scanned objects, whose UV maps are typically optimised to maximise the use of texture-image space and therefore scatter neighbouring 3D faces across disconnected 2D patches~\cite{maggiordomo_texture_2021}. Several remedies have been proposed. Semantic UV mapping~\cite{vermandere_semantic_2024} re-unwraps the object in semantically meaningful chunks so that connected 3D regions remain connected in 2D. Part-aware approaches go further by first decomposing the mesh into geometrically simpler parts, for which standard unwrapping algorithms such as angle-based flattening (\gls{ABF})~\cite{maggiordomo_texture_2021} produce low-distortion charts. \gls{COACD}~\cite{wei_approximate_2022} performs this decomposition through collision-aware approximate convex decomposition and a tree search, producing nearly convex parts that each unwrap cleanly while preserving spatial proximity.

% Paragraph purpose: 3D vertex-color methods (IF-Net Texture) - state limitation
A different family avoids the UV problem entirely by predicting colour directly in 3D. IF-Net Texture~\cite{chibane_implicit_2021} extends IF-Net with per-vertex colour prediction so that the geometry and the appearance are produced together. Because the colour is stored per vertex, the achievable resolution is bounded by the vertex density of the mesh, which on scanned objects is often coarser than the texture-image resolution of UV-based methods, so fine surface detail such as small patterns or sharp colour boundaries is smoothed out (Figure~\ref{fig:7_ifnet_texture}).

\begin{figure}[hbht]
 \centering
 \includegraphics[width=1\textwidth]{IF-Net}
 \caption[IF-Net Texture method overview]{IF-Net Texture~\cite{chibane_implicit_2021}: per-vertex colour is predicted alongside the implicit surface by extending IF-Net with a colour decoder head. The geometry and appearance are produced together, but the resolution of the colour is bounded by the vertex density of the mesh. From \cite{chibane_implicit_2021}}
 \label{fig:7_ifnet_texture}
\end{figure}

% Paragraph purpose: TEXTure - also needs UV but smaller patches
TEXTure~\cite{richardson_texture_2023} sits between the two families: it generates 2D renders of the completed object through a diffusion model and projects them back onto the surface. The projection step still writes into a UV map, but because each render covers only the geometry visible from one view, the texture is composed of many small UV-aligned chunks rather than a single object-wide chart, which lifts the alignment requirement on the unwrap.

% Paragraph purpose: Hunyuan2.0 paint
Image-conditioned generative texturing such as the Hunyuan3D~2.0 paint pipeline~\cite{zhao_hunyuan3d_2025} builds on this multi-view idea and adds three ingredients: a reference-net that preserves appearance details across views, geometry conditioning that ensures the synthesised textures adhere to the surface, and multi-view attention that keeps the result consistent between viewpoints. The output is a baked texture map produced directly from a single input image.


% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\subsection{Material Detection}
\label{sec:7_rw_material}

Most of the texture methods above treat the object as a single texture surface, but real furniture combines several distinct materials. Detecting these materials and reasoning per material rather than per object improves both segmentation boundaries and inpainting quality, because each material provides its own coherent reference region.

In 2D, Segment Anything (SAM)~\cite{kirillov_segment_2023} detects similar visual regions in an image and produces a large set of disjoint masks. Materialistic~\cite{sharma_materialistic_2023} specialises in detecting similar materials in a single image, although with limited granularity, and view-based approaches such as Material-Based Segmentation of Objects~\cite{stets_material-based_2019} segment the object from rendered views. Reflective materials remain difficult because they reflect the environment rather than themselves; Multimodal Material Segmentation~\cite{liang_multimodal_nodate} addresses this by combining RGB, near-infrared and polarised images.

In 3D, TextureNet~\cite{huang_texturenet_2018} encodes the colour of feature points into more distinctive descriptors and uses these for segmentation, which extends naturally to per-material labels within a single object. The voxel-based texture pipeline used later in this chapter follows this 3D direction: it combines an SAM-based 2D segmentation with a 3D classifier that labels every face of the completed mesh with a material index.


% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Voxel-Based Object Completion}
\label{sec:7_voxel}

The voxel-based methodology turns the partial textured mesh and the per-object occlusion grid of Chapter~\ref{ch:5_Scene_Segmentation} into a fully textured completed mesh through a parallel pipeline (Figure~\ref{fig:7_voxel_method}): geometry completion fills the missing voxels with AutoSDF on one track, while material segmentation operates on the partial mesh on a second track concurrently; the two tracks then merge so that IF-Net can extend the material indices onto the new geometry, and a per-material patch-based inpainting can finally fill the missing texture.

\begin{figure}[hbht]
 \centering
 \includegraphics[width=1\textwidth]{Object_completion_voxel_method}
 \caption[Voxel-based object completion pipeline]{Overview of the voxel-based object completion pipeline. The partial point cloud (top left) is converted to a UDF inside the refined bounding box, completed by AutoSDF using the occluded voxels as the to-be-completed selection, then textured through material segmentation and per-material inpainting, resulting in a completed mesh (bottom left).}
 \label{fig:7_voxel_method}
\end{figure}

% Note on input: where the partial textured mesh comes from, since the scanner produces points and not meshes
The partial textured mesh expected by this pipeline is not produced directly by the scanner, since terrestrial laser scanners and mobile mapping systems output unstructured coloured point clouds rather than meshes with a UV map. Two routes are used in practice to obtain it. When multiple aligned images of the object are available within the scene, a photogrammetric reconstruction pipeline yields a textured surface directly. When only the panoramic image is available, the partial point cloud is first meshed through Poisson surface reconstruction~\cite{kazhdan_screened_2013} and the panorama is then projected onto the resulting surface to produce a texture, following the projection scheme already used in Chapter~\ref{ch:6_Scene_Completion}. Either route gives a partial mesh that covers the observed surface and carries an existing UV layout and texture image.


% ── 7.3.1 ────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\subsection{Voxel Preparation}
\label{sec:7_voxel_prep}

% Motivation first
AutoSDF expects two inputs: a voxelised distance field of the partial object and a binary mask of the voxels that need to be completed. The first encodes what was observed; the second tells the network where to invent geometry. Both must be produced before any completion can happen.

\paragraph{Voxel grid and distance field.}
The refined oriented bounding box of each object is divided into a $64^3$ voxel grid $\mathbf{G}$, matching the input resolution of AutoSDF. Because partial scans are inherently non-watertight, the partial mesh is converted to a Unsigned Distance Field $f(\mathbf{x})_\text{udf}$ rather than a standard SDF: any signed convention would require an inside-outside decision that the partial surface cannot reliably provide, as motivated in Chapter~\ref{ch:background}. The UDF is sampled at the centre of each voxel to obtain a per-voxel scalar $g_{ijk} = f(\mathbf{x}_{ijk})_\text{udf}$.

\paragraph{To-be-completed voxel mask.}
% Motivation: prior workflows required manual or symmetry-based mask; the occlusion grid removes this need
In earlier voxel-based completion workflows, the to-be-completed mask had to be defined either by extending the bounding box past the observed surface~\cite{mittal_autosdf_2022} or by hand-picking voxels in a 3D editor~\cite{wegen_interactive_2022, vermandere_guided_2025}. Both options require an extra step that is either coarse or labour-intensive. The per-object occlusion grid produced by the voxelised ray marching of Chapter~\ref{ch:5_Scene_Segmentation} solves this problem directly: every voxel inside the bounding box has already been classified as observed-empty, occupied or occluded, and the occluded voxels are exactly the regions where geometry could still be hidden. A voxel is therefore added to the completion set if its label is \textit{occluded} and it is not already occupied by observed surface. If the occlusion grid was computed at a different resolution from the AutoSDF grid, it is resampled to $64^3$ through nearest-neighbour upsampling so that the two grids align voxel-for-voxel. Because the occlusion grid was computed inside the oriented bounding box rather than along the world axes, the resulting mask is automatically aligned with the object's principal axes.


% ── 7.3.2 ────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\subsection{Geometry Completion}
\label{sec:7_voxel_geom}

% Motivation: the completion is probabilistic; multiple candidates are generated, the best one matching the observed surface is kept
With the UDF and the mask in place, AutoSDF treats the voxel grid as a sequence of tokens, autoregressively predicting the latent codes for the masked voxels conditioned on the observed ones, and decoding the result back to a complete SDF. Because the prediction is sampled rather than deterministic, the network returns a number of candidate completions per input (Figure~\ref{fig:7_voxel_candidates}). This variability is useful for objects with ambiguous geometry (for instance a partially scanned chair whose back is hidden), but it requires a selection step to commit to a single output.

\begin{figure}[hbht]
 \centering
 \includegraphics[width=1\textwidth]{Object_completion_multi_outputs}
 \caption[Multiple candidate completions from AutoSDF]{AutoSDF returns multiple candidate completions per input (top: input UDF, bottom: four candidate completions). The candidate whose decoded surface lies closest to the input UDF zero-level set is retained.}
 \label{fig:7_voxel_candidates}
\end{figure}

The candidate with the smallest mean Euclidean distance between its decoded surface and the input UDF zero-level set is retained, since this is the candidate whose observed parts have been altered the least by the \gls{VQ-VAE} encoding.

% Bridge to next step: SDF -> mesh
The output of the network is a completed SDF on the same $64^3$ grid. Because the next stage of the pipeline operates on explicit triangle geometry, the SDF is converted to a mesh through Marching Cubes~\cite{lorensen_marching_1998}, which extracts the zero level set as a watertight triangulated surface. This meshed result fills the previously occluded region while remaining consistent with the observed geometry at the boundary, and is the input to the texture stage.


% ── 7.3.3 ────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\subsection{Texture Completion}
\label{sec:7_voxel_texture}

% Motivation: state the parallelism with geometry completion and the join structure of the texture stage.
The texture pipeline runs in parallel with the geometry completion of Section~\ref{sec:7_voxel_geom} and merges with it once both intermediate results are available. The first step, material segmentation, operates on the partial mesh and produces a per-face material index on the observed surface (Figure~\ref{fig:7_material_segmentation}). In the second step, the partial material indices are transferred onto the completed mesh and IF-Net extends them onto the completed geometry (Figure~\ref{fig:7_material_prediction}). The third step, texture inpainting, transfers the original partial texture onto the completed mesh and fills the missing pixels material by material (Figure~\ref{fig:7_object_inpainting}).

\paragraph{Material segmentation.}
% Motivation: identify the materials on the observed surface only, before the completed geometry is available.
Material segmentation is performed on the partial input mesh. The goal of the step is to label every observed face with a material index, which the later steps then use to isolate the texture per material.

\begin{wrapfigure}{r}{0.33\textwidth}
 \centering
 \includegraphics[width=0.33\textwidth]{Half_chair_decomposition}
 \caption[COACD convex decomposition]{Convex decomposition of a partial chair mesh with \gls{COACD}; each part is shown in a random colour.}
 \label{fig:7_coacd}
\end{wrapfigure}

% UV unwrapping substep on the partial mesh
The partial mesh is first re-unwrapped on a part-aware UV layout, because the scattered UV charts of typical scanned meshes confuse the segmentation by placing visually distinct material regions next to each other in 2D. To avoid this, the partial mesh is decomposed into nearly convex parts with \gls{COACD}~\cite{wei_approximate_2022} (Figure~\ref{fig:7_coacd}). Each part is geometrically simple enough to be unwrapped without large distortion, so a standard \gls{ABF} unwrap~\cite{maggiordomo_texture_2021} produces a chart in which faces that are adjacent on the 3D part remain adjacent in the UV image. Combined across all parts, the result is a semantic UV layout~\cite{vermandere_semantic_2024} that preserves the spatial proximity of the partial mesh.

% Segmentation + grouping substep on the partial UV
The re-unwrapped partial texture is then segmented into patches using SAM~\cite{kirillov_segment_2023}, which produces a large set of disjoint regions that share visual similarity but does not yet group them by material. Patches that belong to the same material are grouped through the cosine similarity of EfficientNet~\cite{tan_efficientnet_2019} features:

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:7_patch_grouping}
    \begin{gathered}
    \boldsymbol{S} = \bigcup_{i} \left\{ S_i = \{P_i, P_j\} \,\Big|\, \forall P_i, P_j \in \boldsymbol{P} : \frac{f_\text{feat}(P_i) \cdot f_\text{feat}(P_j)}{\|f_\text{feat}(P_i)\| \, \|f_\text{feat}(P_j)\|} \geq t_c \right\}.
    \end{gathered}
\end{equation}

To ensure maximum visual separation, the similarity threshold $t_c$ is tuned so that no more than eight groups remain, since each group is assigned a three-channel material index and can therefore carry $2^3 = 8$ labels. The boundaries of each group are baked into the partial mesh as new edges so that every face of the partial mesh carries a single material index.

\begin{figure}[hbht]
 \centering
 \includegraphics[width=1\textwidth]{Material_segmentation}
 \caption[Material segmentation pipeline]{Material segmentation on the partial mesh: UV unwrapping of the partial mesh (left), full SAM patch segmentation on the unwrapped partial texture (centre), and the final material grouping after EfficientNet feature clustering (right).}
 \label{fig:7_material_segmentation}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{Material prediction.}
% Motivation: this is where the parallel tracks meet; first transfer the observed indices, then extend them with IF-Net.
At this point both parallel tracks have produced their intermediate output: the geometry track has delivered the completed mesh of Section~\ref{sec:7_voxel_geom}, and the segmentation track has delivered the partial mesh labelled with per-face material indices. They are joined in two stages.

First, the material indices are transferred from the partial mesh onto the completed mesh through a 3D-to-3D correspondence. For each face on the completed mesh, the closest face on the partial mesh is found through a k-d tree query: when this closest face lies within a distance threshold (one voxel of $\mathbf{G}$), its material index is copied over; otherwise the face is marked as unlabelled. This populates roughly the half of the completed mesh that came from the original observation, and leaves the AutoSDF-generated half empty.

Second, the unlabelled half is filled in by IF-Net~\cite{chibane_implicit_2021}. Material indices are coarser than the eventual texture: they only need to identify which group a face belongs to, not what its colour is. A per-vertex prediction is therefore sufficient, and the resolution limitation that made IF-Net unsuitable for full texture recovery does not affect material indices. The labelled half of the completed mesh is sampled as a coloured point cloud where each point carries its material index colour, voxelised at the AutoSDF resolution, and passed through the IF-Net encoder. The decoder then predicts the material index at every query point, extending the observed labelling onto the previously unseen regions. The output of this step is the completed mesh with a full per-face material index map.

\begin{figure}[hbht]
 \centering
 \includegraphics[width=1\textwidth]{Material_prediction}
 \caption[IF-Net material index prediction]{Material index prediction: partial material indices transferred onto the completed mesh (left) and the full material labelling after IF-Net extension to the unobserved geometry (right).}
 \label{fig:7_material_prediction}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{Texture inpainting.}
% Motivation: now that material indices live on the completed mesh, transfer the partial texture, re-unwrap the completed mesh on its own layout, and inpaint per material.
The final step transfers the original partial texture onto the completed mesh and fills in the missing pixels material by material. The partial texture is first mapped to the completed mesh through the same 3D-to-3D correspondence that was used to transfer the material indices, so that each face on the completed mesh inherits the partial texture's colour from its closest matching partial face and the AutoSDF-generated regions remain empty.

% Second COACD application, this time on the completed mesh.
The completed mesh is then re-unwrapped on its own UV layout, again through \gls{COACD}~\cite{wei_approximate_2022} followed by \gls{ABF}~\cite{maggiordomo_texture_2021}, this time applied to the completed geometry rather than the partial one. The partial texture and the material indices are projected together into this new UV layout, so the per-material inpainter has both the reference appearance and the per-material mask aligned in the same 2D space.

% Per-material inpainting on the new UV layout
In this new UV layout, the material indices act as masks that isolate one material at a time. For each material set $S_i$, the observed patches $P \in S_i$ act as the reference texture and all other regions are masked out, so the inpainter receives a clean single-material reference instead of a mixed one. The missing texture is then synthesised through patch-based image quilting~\cite{efros_image_2001}: square blocks $\{B_1, B_2, \ldots, B_n\}$ from $S_i$ are stitched together with overlap, and the cut between adjacent blocks is chosen to minimise the cumulative neighbour-contrast error
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:7_inpainting}
    \begin{gathered}
        E_{ij} = e_{ij} + \min(E_{i-1,j-1},\, E_{i-1,j},\, E_{i-1,j+1}),
    \end{gathered}
\end{equation}
with $e_{ij} = f(B_i, B_j)$ the boundary cost between blocks $B_i$ and $B_j$. Each material is inpainted independently, which keeps the boundaries sharp and prevents textures from one material bleeding into another. The result is a fully textured completed mesh.

 \centering
 \includegraphics[width=1\textwidth]{Object_inpainting}
 \caption[Per-material texture inpainting]{Per-material texture inpainting: the completed mesh with the partial texture and material indices transferred onto it (left); the new UV layout produced by re-unwrapping the completed mesh with \gls{COACD} (top centre) before per-material patch-based quilting (bottom centre); the final fully textured completed mesh (right).}

% ── 7.3.4 ────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\subsection{Experiments}
\label{sec:7_voxel_exp}
 
% Motivation for the evaluation choice: AutoSDF was trained on ShapeNet; standard furniture is in-distribution. Real shortcomings only become visible on categories outside that prior, so an industrial dataset is used in addition to Matterport.
The voxel-based method is evaluated on two datasets: a qualitative evaluation on Matterport3D~\cite{chang_matterport3d_2017} to illustrate the typical performance on in-distribution scanned furniture, and a quantitative evaluation on the industrial object dataset introduced in Chapter~\ref{ch:3_Data_Acquisition} to expose how the method behaves outside the AutoSDF training prior.

\begin{figure}[H]

\paragraph{Qualitative results on Matterport3D.}
% Show first: the per-stage figure, then a discussion grounded in observations from each row.
Figure~\ref{fig:7_voxel_matterport} stacks the per-stage outputs of the voxel pipeline for four representative Matterport objects, with each row corresponding to one intermediate result of Sections~\ref{sec:7_voxel_geom}--\ref{sec:7_voxel_texture}: the partial input mesh, the AutoSDF-completed geometry, the completed material indices in 3D, the UV layout with the projected partial texture and the per-material inpainting mask, the inpainted texture, and the final fully textured mesh.
 
% Geometry observations
The second row shows that AutoSDF predicts large missing regions consistently and returns clean, dense meshes with well-defined boundaries, even on objects whose ground truth covers only a fraction of the volume. The same row also shows the cost: fine surface detail is smoothed out by the $64^3$ voxelisation, and originally observed parts of the mesh are slightly altered by the \gls{VQ-VAE} encoding even though they were never marked as to-be-completed~\cite{vermandere_geometry_2025}. The candidate selection of Section~\ref{sec:7_voxel_geom} mitigates the latter by retaining the variant that lies closest to the partial surface.
 
% Material segmentation observations
The third row shows that SAM groups basic materials such as wood, fabric and plastic into clean patches regardless of orientation, and that the EfficientNet similarity step recovers the few dominant materials per object. Two failure modes appear on the harder cases: small patches drop below the resolution at which SAM can recognise them as a single material, and baked-in lighting (shadows under armrests, highlights on glossy plastic) splits what should be a single material into several similarity groups, the same issue reported on textured reconstructions in~\cite{siddiqui_retrievalfuse_2021}.
 
% Material prediction observations
The third row also demonstrates the effect of the IF-Net extension: material boundaries on the AutoSDF-generated geometry are coherent with the observed ones, even when the unobserved region covers a sizeable fraction of the object. The prediction degrades only when the partial labelling contains many distinct materials or when the unobserved region is so large that no nearby labelled face is available; in those cases the network defaults to the dominant material of the object, which is visible on the more occluded examples.
 
% Texture inpainting observations
The fourth and fifth rows show the per-material inpainting at work. Well-scanned objects such as the sofa and the stool yield consistent geometry and texture completions, since the reference patches are large enough for patch-based quilting to stitch a coherent surface. Less-scanned objects still produce plausible geometry, but the inpainting becomes repetitive when the reference patch is small relative to the missing region, and struggles on irregular patterns such as printed graphics. Unlike approaches that limit segmentation to predefined material classes~\cite{stets_material-based_2019}, the generic material labelling used here keeps the inpainting independent of the actual material identity, but in doing so it shifts the difficulty entirely onto the 2D inpainter.

The synthetic regime measures whether the network can learn the industrial classes at all when given clean inputs, the real regime measures whether the result transfers to scanned data.

On the synthetic counterpart of the dataset (Table~\ref{tab:7_industrial}), the IoU sits in the $60$--$75\,\%$ range expected for SDF-based completion methods on their own training distribution~\cite{rao_patchcomplete_2022, chu_diffcomplete_2023}, and performance degrades only when more than $70\,\%$ of the volume must be predicted from less than $30\,\%$ of observed geometry. Round objects benefit most from fine-tuning because their surfaces are smoother and more predictable than the bends and fittings of rectangular pipework, while rectangular pipes drop more sharply at the highest completeness intervals because their right-angle features are easily perturbed by the latent-code substitution.
 
% Real-scan results: pipes
On the real scanned pipe segments, the synthetic numbers do not transfer. Figure~\ref{fig:7_voxel_pipe_industrial} shows the typical failure mode: the bottom half of the input is reproduced more or less correctly, but the upper half, which the network must predict, is replaced by recognisable fragments of furniture from the ShapeNet prior, such as chair backs or sofa shapes for the round-rectangular junctions, and a small aeroplane fuselage for the long straight rectangular pipe. The fine-tuning was not enough to override the dominant prior, and the network falls back to the closest categories it knows whenever the partial input is ambiguous.

The storage vessels exhibit a second, distinct failure mode (Figure~\ref{fig:7_voxel_vessel_industrial}). After fine-tuning, the per-vessel IoU still spans $27\,\%$ to $97\,\%$, but the Chamfer distance systematically increases with respect to the partial input rather than decreasing, and the F-score and coverage both drop after every completion, indicating that the network is adding more incorrect surface than correct surface. The cause is the noise floor of the scans: reflective metal surfaces and open vessel tops scatter the laser pulse, leaving the partial UDF with internal artefacts that AutoSDF was never trained to ignore. Because the network was trained exclusively on synthetic, clean inputs, it interprets the noise as geometry and reconstructs around it.

The goal of the industrial experiment was to verify whether the voxel-based method, after dedicated fine-tuning on a synthetic counterpart, could be applied to industrial geometry. The synthetic results are reasonable, but the two real-scan failures show that the method does not transfer: the ShapeNet prior dominates when the input is ambiguous, and clean-input training leaves the network unable to ignore scan noise. Together with the eight-material cap on the texture side and the \gls{VQ-VAE}-induced alteration of observed geometry, this motivates the image-conditioned method developed next.

\section{Image-Based Object Completion}
\label{sec:7_image}

% Motivation: a fundamentally different approach. The image carries the high-level shape and category cues; the partial point cloud anchors the result to the observed surface; the voxel-based pipeline's failure modes come from relying on geometry alone, which the image side fixes.
The image-based methodology is a fundamentally different approach to the same problem. Instead of treating the partial geometry as the primary signal and inferring the rest from a learned voxel prior, it treats an image of the object as the primary signal and uses the partial point cloud only as a geometric anchor. The image carries the category, the shape and the appearance in a single dense representation, which lets the generator reason about parts that the scan could not capture at all. The pipeline (Figure~\ref{fig:7_image_method}) has three stages: a cropped pinhole image of the object is rendered from the panorama, Hunyuan3D-Omni~\cite{hunyuan3d_hunyuan3d-omni_2025} produces the completed mesh from the image and the partial point cloud, and the Hunyuan3D~2.0 paint pipeline~\cite{zhao_hunyuan3d_2025} adds the texture from the same image.

% ── 7.4.1 ────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\subsection{Image Cropping}
\label{sec:7_image_crop}

% Motivation, more descriptive: WHY do we crop, and HOW (pinhole re-projection rather than masking)
The panoramic image of Chapter~\ref{ch:6_Scene_Completion} captures the entire surrounding scene, but the completion network needs a view in which the target object is dominant and unrelated geometry is absent. Feeding the full panorama would force the network to also reason about furniture, walls and clutter that have no bearing on the object at hand. The bounding-box corners produced in Chapter~\ref{ch:5_Scene_Segmentation} bound the volume the object occupies, so they also bound the angular extent that the object occupies in the panorama, which is exactly the information needed to compute a focused view.

% Step 1: project corners to panorama pixel coordinates
The eight corners $\mathbf{p}_i = (x_i, y_i, z_i)^\top$ of the refined oriented bounding box are first expressed in the panorama camera frame as $\mathbf{p}_i^c = \mathbf{R}(\mathbf{p}_i - \vec{\mathbf{t}})$, using the same scanner transform $\mathbf{T} = [\mathbf{R} \mid \vec{\mathbf{t}}]$ that placed the panorama in world coordinates. The camera-frame coordinates are converted to spherical coordinates
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:7_spherical}
    \begin{gathered}
    \theta_i = \operatorname{atan2}(x_i^c,\, z_i^c), \qquad
    \phi_i = \arcsin\!\left(\frac{y_i^c}{\|\mathbf{p}_i^c\|}\right),
    \end{gathered}
\end{equation}
and finally to equirectangular pixel coordinates for an image of width $W$ and height $H$:
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:7_equirectangular}
    \begin{gathered}
    u_i = W \left( \frac{\theta_i + \pi}{2\pi} \right), \qquad
    v_i = H \left( \frac{\pi/2 - \phi_i}{\pi} \right).
    \end{gathered}
\end{equation}

% Motivation: pinhole reprojection rather than rectangular masking
% Step 2: convert footprint to pinhole FoV and reproject
The eight projected coordinates $\mathbf{c}_i = (u_i, v_i)$ form a polygon on the panorama; a simple axis-aligned crop of this polygon would still produce an equirectangular-distorted image, which is not how natural views are taken and not what the completion network was trained on. Instead, the angular bounds of the eight corners define the horizontal and vertical \gls{FoV} of a pinhole camera placed at the scanner position and oriented towards the centre of the object. The panorama is then resampled through this pinhole model, producing an undistorted image that frames the object as if it had been photographed in isolation by a virtual camera at the scanner location (Figure~\ref{fig:7_pinhole_crop}). This view is paired with a transparency mask derived from the projected bounding-box polygon to suppress what remains of the surrounding scene at the edges of the crop.

% ── 7.4.2 ────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\subsection{Geometry Generation}
\label{sec:7_image_geom}

% Motivation: how we use the network, not what the network is
The cropped pinhole view and the partial point cloud of the object are passed to Hunyuan3D-Omni as the image condition and the point condition respectively. The point cloud is downsampled to the network's expected input size and re-oriented to the canonical frame used during training, since the network assumes a fixed up-axis. The image is fed unaltered.

% Motivation: why the point cloud is essential here, not in the thesis as a whole
The role of the partial point cloud is to anchor the generated geometry to the observed surface. Without the point condition the network would still produce a plausible object from the image alone, but the result would not be aligned with the scanned data and could drift in scale, position or proportion. With the point condition, the generated SDF is conditioned to pass through the observed points, which plays the same role that the occluded-voxel mask played for AutoSDF: it tells the network which part of the answer is already fixed.

% Output of this step
The network outputs an SDF on an adaptive octree at up to $256^3$ resolution, from which a mesh is extracted at the zero level set through Marching Cubes~\cite{lorensen_marching_1998}. Two cleanup passes are then applied: floater removal eliminates small disconnected components that the decoder sometimes leaves outside the main shape, and degenerate-face removal collapses zero-area triangles produced at the iso-surface. The final output of this step is a watertight, untextured mesh of the complete object, aligned with the partial point cloud and ready to be textured.


% ── 7.4.3 ────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\subsection{Texture Generation}
\label{sec:7_image_texture}

% Motivation: same image, used twice
The Hunyuan3D~2.0 paint pipeline~\cite{zhao_hunyuan3d_2025} takes two inputs: the completed mesh from the previous step and the same cropped pinhole image used for geometry generation. Reusing the image keeps the texture consistent with the geometry it produced, and removes the need to find a separate appearance reference.

The pipeline proceeds in three stages. The image is first passed through a delighting module that removes baked-in highlights and shadows, producing a uniformly lit reference that is independent of the original scanner lighting. A geometry-conditioned multi-view synthesis module then renders the delit image onto the completed mesh from a series of learned camera viewpoints, using a reference-net to preserve appearance details across views, geometry conditioning to ensure that the textures adhere to the surface structure, and multi-view attention to maintain consistency between viewpoints. Finally, the generated multi-view images are baked into a single texture map, with an inpainting step that fills small uncovered regions by propagating information from nearby textured patches.

% Bring the object back to its place in the scene
The completed and textured object is generated in the canonical frame used by Hunyuan3D-Omni. To place it back in the scene at its original location, the bounding-box transformation recorded during Chapter~\ref{ch:5_Scene_Segmentation} is inverted and applied to the output mesh, returning the object to the position, orientation and scale it had in the original scan.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:7_conclusion}

This chapter presented two object completion methodologies that take the partial textured meshes, the per-object occlusion grids and the panoramic image of the preceding chapters as input and produce fully textured object meshes as output.

The voxel-based method uses the per-object occlusion grid of Chapter~\ref{ch:5_Scene_Segmentation} directly as the to-be-completed voxel selection for AutoSDF~\cite{mittal_autosdf_2022, vermandere_guided_2025}, removing the manual or symmetry-based mask construction required by earlier voxel-based workflows. The texture is recovered through a part-aware UV unwrapping based on \gls{COACD}~\cite{wei_approximate_2022} and semantic UV mapping~\cite{vermandere_semantic_2024}, a SAM~\cite{kirillov_segment_2023} segmentation of the partial texture into material groups, an IF-Net~\cite{chibane_implicit_2021} extension of the material indices over the new surface, and a per-material patch-based inpainting~\cite{efros_image_2001}. Quantitative experiments on the synthetic part of the industrial dataset of~\cite{decrocq_voxel_2025} show IoU values between $61.8\,\%$ and $72.2\,\%$ across all completeness intervals, in line with state-of-the-art SDF-based completion. On the real scans of the same dataset, scan noise from reflective surfaces causes the network to reconstruct around the noise and the Chamfer distance increases after completion. These failures, together with the eight-material cap and the partial alteration of observed geometry by the \gls{VQ-VAE} encoding, motivate the second method.

The image-based method shifts the primary input from geometry to imagery. A pinhole view of the object is rendered from the panorama using the bounding-box corners as field-of-view bounds, and is passed to Hunyuan3D-Omni~\cite{hunyuan3d_hunyuan3d-omni_2025} together with the partial point cloud as a geometric anchor. The Hunyuan3D~2.0 paint pipeline~\cite{zhao_hunyuan3d_2025} then textures the result from the same image. By replacing the fixed voxel prior with a large-scale image-conditioned generator, the method generalises across a broader range of objects without per-category fine-tuning and removes the eight-material cap of the voxel-based pipeline, at the cost of a less direct link to the per-object occlusion grid.

Together with the completed environment from Chapter~\ref{ch:6_Scene_Completion}, the textured objects produced here form the static reconstruction of the indoor scene. They are passed on to Chapter~\ref{ch:8_Scene_Dynamification}, where the static meshes are turned into dynamic, part-aware and rescalable elements~\cite{vermandere_adaptive_2026}, completing the transition from a partial scan to an editable 3D environment.
