\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:8_intro}

% Paragraph purpose: situate the chapter as the final pipeline stage and frame it by building-element class.
This chapter addresses the final stage of the pipeline, in which the static reconstructions produced by the preceding chapters are turned into a single interactive \gls{DRM}. The completed environment of Chapter~\ref{ch:6_Scene_Completion} and the completed objects of Chapter~\ref{ch:7_Object_Completion} are geometrically accurate but rigid: their walls are closed, their openings are baked into flat texture, and their objects cannot be resized without distorting their structural proportions~\cite{vermandere_adaptive_2026}. Dynamification removes this rigidity across the three classes of building element introduced in Chapter~\ref{ch:background}. The primary elements, the structural shell of walls, floor and ceiling, are made adjustable so the room envelope can be reshaped. The secondary elements, the windows and doors that the scene completion left hidden inside the inpainted wall textures, are recovered as separable, movable components. The tertiary elements, the furniture and loose objects, are converted into part-aware, selectively scalable entities. The result is a scene in which every element can be manipulated after reconstruction rather than a fixed reconstruction of a single moment (Figure~\ref{fig:8_intro}).

\begin{figure}[hbht]
 \centering
 \includegraphics[width=1\textwidth]{Scene_dynamification_intro}
 \caption[Scene dynamification introduction]{The static reconstructed scene (left) is converted into a dynamic representation (right) in which the structural shell is adjustable, openings are separable, and objects support part-aware scaling.}
 \label{fig:8_intro}
\end{figure}

% Paragraph purpose: state scope, what is reused and what is newly created.
The scene-level dynamification reuses the per-plane reconstructed and inpainted meshes of Chapter~\ref{ch:6_Scene_Completion}, and the object-level dynamification reuses the completion-network output of Chapter~\ref{ch:7_Object_Completion}. Beyond recombining these into one coherent dynamic scene, the chapter introduces two pieces of new geometry and texture. At the scene level, windows and doors are separated from the walls and the surface behind them is reconstructed and re-inpainted, so the wall stays complete when an opening is moved. At the object level, the static completion output is converted into the \gls{CSDF} representation on which the deformation operates. These steps are detailed in the methodology.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:8_related}

This section reviews the four areas specific to dynamification: window and door detection, part segmentation, surface-based deformation and implicit-field deformation.


% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\subsection{Window and Door Detection}
\label{sec:8_rw_detection}

Recovering the openings hidden in the wall textures requires detecting windows and doors in an image without a fixed, pre-trained class set, since the texture content varies widely across rooms and datasets. Open-vocabulary detectors address this by conditioning detection on a free-text prompt. Grounding DINO~\cite{liu_grounding_2024} couples a transformer detector with grounded language pre-training, so that arbitrary phrases such as \emph{window} or \emph{door} can be localised in an image without category-specific training. To turn a detected box into a precise region outline, the Segment Anything Model (SAM)~\cite{kirillov_segment_2023} produces a tight mask from a box or point prompt and generalises across domains without fine-tuning. Combining a prompt-driven detector with a promptable segmenter provides the class-agnostic, mask-level localisation needed to cut openings out of the reconstructed walls, which is the combination adopted in this chapter.


% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\subsection{Part Segmentation}
\label{sec:8_rw_segmentation}

To deform an object part by part, it must first be divided into parts. Learning-based shape segmentation methods predict a semantic or instance label per point. CSN~\cite{loizou_cross-shape_2023} introduces a cross-shape attention mechanism that lets the point features of one shape interact with those of others, improving the consistency of the predicted segmentation across a collection. Mid-Net~\cite{wang_unsupervised_2020} learns a generic shape encoding from unlabelled point clouds by jointly encoding shape and point features, and FG-Net~\cite{liu_fg-net_2020} processes large-scale point clouds directly without voxelisation through correlated feature mining and deformable convolutions. These methods target the semantic decomposition of objects, which is difficult to generalise to the arbitrary, partially reconstructed objects that arrive from Chapter~\ref{ch:7_Object_Completion}, where class labels are unreliable and the part vocabulary is open-ended.

Approximate convex decomposition (ACD) sidesteps the semantic question by splitting a shape into nearly convex components on purely geometric grounds, which is sufficient for defining deformation regions. Classical methods such as HACD~\cite{mamou_simple_2009} cluster mesh faces hierarchically using a concavity-driven merge heuristic, which is robust but tends to produce redundant and overlapping parts. Learning-based approaches such as CvxNet~\cite{deng_cvxnet_2020} represent a shape as a union of learned convex primitives, yielding compact decompositions but generalising poorly outside the training distribution. CoACD~\cite{wei_approximate_2022} cuts the triangle mesh directly with planes, scores cuts with a collision-aware concavity metric that is sensitive to interior geometry, and searches cut sequences through tree search rather than greedy splitting, producing intersection-free convex parts with fewer components. Although the resulting parts do not necessarily coincide with semantic components, their granularity and class-agnostic behaviour make them well suited to defining scaling regions on arbitrary reconstructed objects, which is why CoACD is adopted in this chapter.


% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\subsection{Surface-Based Deformation}
\label{sec:8_rw_surface}

A second family of methods deforms the explicit surface directly. Nine-slicing~\cite{w3_css-backgrounds-3border-image-slice_2024} divides a 2D image into a grid so that corner regions stay fixed while edge and centre regions stretch, and its 3D extension, 27-slicing~\cite{deftly_27_2021}, applies the same idea to a bounding box so that an object can be scaled without distorting its corners. Both rely on manually defined zones and impose no constraint on the deformed region, which restricts them to regular, box-like shapes. This makes them a natural fit for the rectangular framing of windows and doors, where the frame should keep its thickness while the panels stretch, and 27-slicing is used for exactly that purpose in this chapter.

KeypointDeformer~\cite{jakab_keypointdeformer_2021} replaces the manual zones with automatically detected keypoints that drive a cage deformation, producing more natural transformations for moderate edits but degrading under large deformations. Skeleton-driven procedural methods such as that of Getto et al.~\cite{getto_automatic_2020} detect object components from a skeleton to bound the deformation regions, improving results on more complex shapes, but they share the limitation that a stretched part is simply rescaled to fit the target size with no notion of part-size consistency. Proc-GS~\cite{li_proc-gs_2024} instead handles large deformations by treating buildings as assemblies of modular components that can be repeated rather than stretched, but it depends on a predefined library of parts. The repetition-based strategy used in this chapter borrows the idea of repeating modular components from Proc-GS while removing the dependence on a curated part library, deriving the repeatable units directly from the convex decomposition of the object instead~\cite{vermandere_adaptive_2026}.


% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\subsection{Implicit-Field Deformation}
\label{sec:8_rw_implicit}

The objects of Chapter~\ref{ch:7_Object_Completion} are completed as \glspl{SDF} and only meshed in a later stage, so the deformation can act directly on the implicit field before that meshing step, avoiding a detour through an explicit surface. DIF-Net~\cite{deng_deformed_2021} represents a category of shapes with a shared template field together with per-instance deformation and correction fields, enabling non-destructive editing within a learned shape space. SALAD~\cite{koo_salad_2023} uses a part-level latent diffusion model for generating and editing shapes, DualSDF~\cite{hao_dualsdf_2020} exposes a coarse and a fine level of the same \gls{SDF} for semantic manipulation, and HybridSDF~\cite{vasu_hybridsdf_2022} combines implicit shapes with parametric primitives to balance flexibility against structural coherence. For part-level editing, SPAGHETTI~\cite{hertz_spaghetti_2022} supports affine transformations of implicit parts such as rotation and translation with smooth blending, and SENS~\cite{binninger_sens_2024} extends it with sketch-based control, but free scaling at the sub-part level, in particular large scaling that should preserve rather than stretch repeated structure, remains difficult and typically depends on category-specific priors. The method in this chapter targets exactly this gap: it deforms the \gls{CSDF} field of an arbitrary completed object through direct interpolation and repetition, without requiring a learned shape space or a predefined part database~\cite{vermandere_adaptive_2026}.

\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:8_methodology}

The dynamification is organised into a scene-level stage and an object-level stage. The scene-level stage (Section~\ref{sec:8_scene}, Figure~\ref{fig:8_method_scene_overview}) reopens the closed structural shell by detecting windows and doors, separating them as dynamic elements and reconstructing the surface behind them. The object-level stage (Section~\ref{sec:8_object}, Figure~\ref{fig:8_method_object_overview}) converts each completed object into a \gls{CSDF}, decomposes it into convex parts, and scales user-defined regions of the field. The recombination of both into a single interactive scene is described in Section~\ref{sec:8_recombination}.

% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\subsection{Scene Dynamification}
\label{sec:8_scene}

The scene completion of Chapter~\ref{ch:6_Scene_Completion} reconstructs each room as a set of closed walls whose surface is projected onto the scanned points, so a window or door is reconstructed with the geometry of the wall plane fitted to its surrounding points and is distinguishable only by its texture. Scene dynamification recovers these openings as independent, manipulable geometry. Each opening is detected in the wall texture and cut out as a separate surface, which therefore already carries the projected geometry of the opening. It is then turned into a dynamic element with its own frame and panels, and the wall surface behind it is reconstructed as a flat plane so that the structural shell stays complete (Figure~\ref{fig:8_method_scene_overview}).



% ── 8.3.1.1 ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\subsubsection{Opening Detection}
\label{sec:8_opening_detection}

\begin{wrapfigure}{r}{0.3\textwidth}
 \centering
 \vspace{-10pt}
 \includegraphics[width=0.3\textwidth]{Scene_dynamification_opening_detection}
 \caption[Window and door detection on wall textures]{The final detection polygon traced on a flattened wall texture, annotated with its class label and confidence score.}
 \label{fig:8_opening_detection}
\end{wrapfigure}

Each wall fragment $M_k$ produced in Chapter~\ref{ch:6_Scene_Completion} carries a flattened texture in its own \gls{UV} space, which already presents the surface upright and free of perspective distortion. This is the ideal input for a 2D detector, because each window and door appears as a flat, front-facing rectangle rather than a tilted and stretched shape seen from an angle. The base-colour texture of each fragment is extracted and passed to the Grounding DINO~\cite{liu_grounding_2024} open-vocabulary detector, prompted with the phrases \emph{window} and \emph{door}, which returns a bounding box and class label per opening. Each box is then refined into a tight binary mask by SAM~\cite{kirillov_segment_2023}, prompted with that box, and the largest external contour of the mask is traced and simplified into a pixel-space polygon $\{(x_l, y_l)\}$ describing the opening outline (Figure~\ref{fig:8_opening_detection}).


Operating per fragment, in its canonical \gls{UV} orientation, keeps the detection robust: each wall is seen in isolation and upright, so the detector is not confused by adjacent surfaces or by the curvature of the original panorama. A small padding is applied to each detection so that window and door frames, which sit at the very edge of the detected box, are included in the subsequent cutout.


% ── 8.3.1.2 ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\subsubsection{Opening Cutout}
\label{sec:8_opening_cutout}

Each detected polygon is lifted from texture space back onto the wall mesh to extract the opening as a separate surface. The pixel polygon is first converted to \gls{UV} coordinates by normalising each vertex by the texture dimensions, $\mathbf{c}_l = (x_l / W,\, y_l / H)$. The polygon is expanded by a small margin in texture space so that the cut includes the full frame.

The polygon is then used to slice every triangle of the wall fragment in \gls{UV} space: each mesh triangle is intersected with the opening polygon and the part falling inside is re-triangulated. The new vertices created along the cut receive interpolated \gls{UV} coordinates from the original triangle, so the extracted surface still samples the original wall texture and lies exactly on the wall plane. Concretely, a new vertex with barycentric weights $(b_0, b_1, b_2)$ relative to the original triangle vertices $\mathbf{v}_0, \mathbf{v}_1, \mathbf{v}_2$ takes the 3D position

\[
\mathbf{x} = b_0\,\mathbf{v}_0 + b_1\,\mathbf{v}_1 + b_2\,\mathbf{v}_2 .
\]

The union of all clipped triangles forms a new textured mesh containing only the opening region, separated from the wall but geometrically and texturally consistent with it (Figure~\ref{fig:8_opening_cutout}). All geometric detail of the opening, such as the recess of the frame and the glazing, is retained on this cutout surface rather than on the wall.

\begin{figure}[hbht]
 \centering
 \includegraphics[width=1\textwidth]{Scene_dynamification_opening_cutout}
 \caption[Opening cutout from a wall mesh]{The detected opening polygon (left) is clipped out of the wall mesh triangle by triangle, producing a separate window or door surface (right) that keeps the original texture and plane.}
 \label{fig:8_opening_cutout}
\end{figure}


% ── 8.3.1.3 ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\subsubsection{Dynamic Opening Construction}
\label{sec:8_opening_dynamic}

The cutout already carries the projected geometry of the opening, but its outer edge sits exactly on the wall plane, which would leave a gap when the element is moved or scaled. To make it behave as a real, movable opening, its outer perimeter is extruded back toward the wall so the edges meet the surface cleanly and the element gains the volume of a frame (Figure~\ref{fig:8_opening_dynamic}). A boolean bounding box is then built around this volume and used to procedurally remove the part of the wall it intersects: rather than re-meshing the wall, the intersection is carved at render time through a screen-space stencil-buffer boolean, which keeps the operation interactive and free of geometric artefacts as the opening is moved or resized. Finally, the opening is 27-sliced~\cite{deftly_27_2021} using user-defined boundaries that can be placed to follow the framing and glazing, so that resizing stretches only the panes and infill while the frame keeps its real thickness, matching how a physical window or door scales.

\begin{figure}[hbht]
 \centering
 \includegraphics[width=1\textwidth]{Scene_dynamification_opening_dynamic}
 \caption[Construction of a dynamic opening]{A 27-sliced window cutout (left) and the same opening after scaling (right): the panes stretch to the new size while the framing keeps its original thickness.}
 \label{fig:8_opening_dynamic}
\end{figure}


% ── 8.3.1.4 ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\subsubsection{Wall Re-Reconstruction}
\label{sec:8_wall_reinpaint}

Because windows and doors are now treated as separate, movable elements, the wall must be complete underneath them so that an opening can be repositioned or removed without exposing a gap. The wall surface behind each opening is reconstructed as a flat region in the same plane as the surrounding wall: rather than reproducing the recessed geometry of the original opening, which now lives entirely on the cutout, the patch is kept planar so it matches the neighbouring wall and floor surfaces. Its texture is completed with the same per-plane strategy used in Chapter~\ref{ch:6_Scene_Completion}: the opening polygons are rasterised into an alpha mask on the wall texture, marking the opening interior as the region to fill, and the masked texture is completed by LaMa~\cite{suvorov_resolution-robust_2022} so that the wall continues plausibly behind the removed opening (Figure~\ref{fig:8_wall_reinpaint}). The wall mesh is closed across the hole using the same constrained triangulation as in Chapter~\ref{ch:6_Scene_Completion}, leaving a continuous structural surface with the opening represented as an independent overlay on top of it.

% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\subsection{Object Dynamification}
\label{sec:8_object}

Depending on the completion method of Chapter~\ref{ch:7_Object_Completion}, each completed object arrives either as a textured mesh or as a mesh extracted from a completed \gls{SDF} with a texture applied. Object dynamification first brings both forms into a single deformable representation, the \gls{CSDF}, then turns it into a part-aware, selectively scalable field through convex decomposition, scaling-zone definition, joint interpolation, and, for large deformations, repetition of modular parts (Figure~\ref{fig:8_method_object_overview}).



% ── 8.3.2.1 ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\subsubsection{Part Segmentation}
\label{sec:8_part_segmentation}

\begin{wrapfigure}{r}{0.4\textwidth}
 \centering
 \includegraphics[width=0.4\textwidth]{Scene_dynamification_decomposition}
 \caption[Convex decomposition of a completed object]{The approximately convex decomposition produced by CoACD~\cite{wei_approximate_2022}, with each part shown in a random colour.}
 \label{fig:8_decomposition}
\end{wrapfigure}

Part segmentation is performed first on the completed mesh, because it produces an extra per-voxel attribute that is stored in the \gls{CSDF} alongside geometry and colour. The mesh is decomposed into approximately convex components using CoACD~\cite{wei_approximate_2022}, which recursively cuts the mesh with planes selected through a tree search that minimises a collision-aware concavity metric (Figure~\ref{fig:8_decomposition}). Adjacent components are merged where their union remains convex, reducing redundant cuts. Because the decomposition is purely geometric, it generalises to arbitrary reconstructed objects without relying on a class label, at the cost of parts that do not always coincide with semantic components.



Each component is assigned a discrete part index $i$, which is later sampled onto the voxel grid so that every voxel knows which structural component it belongs to. These indices drive the part-aware deformation in Section~\ref{sec:8_interpolation}.


% ── 8.3.2.2 ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\subsubsection{CSDF Creation}
\label{sec:8_csdf}

The completed mesh, its texture and its part labels are unified into a single \gls{CSDF} (Figure~\ref{fig:8_csdf}). A signed distance value $f(\mathbf{x})_\text{sdf}$ is sampled on a regular $128 \times 128 \times 128$ voxel grid, and the object texture is sampled onto the same grid so that each voxel also stores a colour $\mathbf{C} = (R, G, B)$. The part index $i$ from Section~\ref{sec:8_part_segmentation} is transferred to the grid by nearest-neighbour assignment, adding a third attribute per voxel. The grid is exported to a 3D texture sliced one pixel per voxel, where the RGB channels hold the colour and the alpha channel holds the signed distance value, with the part index carried in an auxiliary channel. This packed representation encodes geometry, appearance and structure in one volume and is the unit on which all subsequent deformation operates~\cite{vermandere_adaptive_2026}.

\begin{figure}[hbht]
 \centering
 \includegraphics[width=1\textwidth]{Scene_dynamification_csdf}
 \caption[CSDF creation]{The \gls{CSDF} sliced into an $8\times4$ grid of $64\times64$ pixel slices (left) and the corresponding 3D rendering of the packed volume (right). The RGB channels hold the colour, the alpha channel the signed distance value, and an auxiliary channel the part index.}
 \label{fig:8_csdf}
\end{figure}


% ── 8.3.2.3 ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\subsubsection{Scaling-Zone Definition}
\label{sec:8_scaling_zone}

\begin{wrapfigure}[16]{r}{0.45\textwidth}
 \centering
 \vspace{-50pt}
 \includegraphics[width=0.4\textwidth]{Scene_dynamification_scaling_zone}
 \caption[Scaling-zone definition]{A scaling zone defined on a chair by a start plane (blue) and an end plane (red) along the scaling axis.}
 \label{fig:8_scaling_zone}
\end{wrapfigure}

Selective scaling is controlled by a simple planar constraint. A scaling zone is delimited by two parallel planes along a chosen axis, a start plane and an end plane, and the end plane is translated along that axis to set the target extent of the zone (Figure~\ref{fig:8_scaling_zone}). Voxels before the start plane stay fixed, voxels beyond the end plane are rigidly translated to keep the rest of the object intact, and the voxels between the two planes are the ones that are deformed. This planar control is intuitive and behaves identically across objects, independent of their shape or class.


% ── 8.3.2.4 ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\subsubsection{Joint Field Interpolation}
\label{sec:8_interpolation}

Within the scaling zone, a new voxel grid is allocated at the target extent and the field is resampled into it, jointly updating geometry, colour and part indices (Figure~\ref{fig:8_interpolation}). The part index controls which voxels take part in the deformation: rather than scaling only the voxels strictly between the two planes, every part that is intersected by the planes is scaled as a whole. This grows the selection out to the natural boundaries of the sliced components, so the deformation follows the structure of the object instead of an arbitrary planar cut and produces a more natural-looking selection of voxels.

\begin{figure}[hbht]
 \centering
 \includegraphics[width=1\textwidth]{Scene_dynamification_interpolation}
 \caption[Joint interpolation within a scaling zone]{Joint interpolation of the signed distance field, colour and part indices within a scaling zone, shown for a vertical scaling (left) and a horizontal scaling (right) of the same object.}
 \label{fig:8_interpolation}
\end{figure}

The signed distance and colour are continuous quantities and are interpolated linearly along the scaling axis. To limit computation and memory, interpolation is restricted to voxels near the surface, and the signed distance is locally re-evaluated after interpolation so that the zero level set remains consistent and free of discontinuities.

The part index $i$ is discrete and cannot be interpolated. Instead, the boundary between two adjacent parts is located along the scaling axis at the position where the index changes and the surface is closest, so that part transitions are preserved through the deformation rather than blurred:

\[
x_b = \argmin_{x} \big( |f(x)_\text{sdf}| \;\text{ where }\; i(x) \neq i(x + \delta x) \big),
\]

where $\delta x$ is a small sampling offset used to detect the label change.


% ── 8.3.2.5 ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\subsubsection{Repetition-Based Scaling}
\label{sec:8_repetition}

\begin{wrapfigure}{r}{0.4\textwidth}
 \centering
 \vspace{-30pt}
 \includegraphics[width=0.4\textwidth]{Scene_dynamification_repetition}
 \caption[Repetition-based scaling]{For repeating structures, the selected region (left) is duplicated rather than stretched (right), preserving the proportions of each component during large deformations.}
 \label{fig:8_repetition}
\end{wrapfigure}

For large deformations, stretching a region introduces visible distortion: shelves grow too tall, cushions become elongated, and repeated elements lose their proportions. For regions whose parts form a repeating pattern, the components are duplicated instead of stretched (Figure~\ref{fig:8_repetition}). Given an original repeatable length $L$ and a target length $L'$, the number of repetitions is

\[
n = \left\lfloor \frac{L'}{L} \right\rfloor,
\]

and the region is duplicated $n$ times along the scaling axis. The residual difference between $n L$ and $L'$ is absorbed by slightly scaling each repeated section using the interpolation scheme of Section~\ref{sec:8_interpolation}, and the boundary layers between adjacent repetitions are blended to avoid visible seams. This preserves the size of individual components while still reaching the target dimension~\cite{vermandere_adaptive_2026}.

All of these deformations are applied in real time. The packed \gls{CSDF} is never edited destructively; it serves as the interpolation source from which the deformed field is sampled on the fly, and the result is continuously visualised as a surface extracted with Marching Cubes~\cite{lorensen_marching_1998}. The deformed grid is only written back to a 3D texture, optionally resampled to a fixed output resolution, when the object is exported.


% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\subsection{Recombination into a Dynamic Scene}
\label{sec:8_recombination}

The dynamified openings and objects are placed back into the completed environment to form a single interactive scene in the Unity game engine (Figure~\ref{fig:8_recombination}). The structural shell and the openings are kept as explicit meshes, while each scalable object is visualised by extracting its surface from the \gls{CSDF} with Marching Cubes. This surface is only recomputed when the field itself changes, that is when an object is scaled; an object that is merely moved reuses its existing mesh, since its geometry, colour and part structure are unchanged. The scaled objects are positioned at the locations recovered during object isolation in Chapter~\ref{ch:5_Scene_Segmentation}, and the separated windows and doors remain as movable overlays on the re-reconstructed walls of Section~\ref{sec:8_wall_reinpaint}.

\begin{figure}[hbht]
 \centering
 \includegraphics[width=1\textwidth]{Scene_dynamification_recombination}
 \caption[Recombination into a dynamic scene]{The dynamified openings and objects are recombined with the completed environment into a single interactive scene in Unity, where the structural shell, openings and objects can all be manipulated.}
 \label{fig:8_recombination}
\end{figure}

All three element classes are manipulated interactively from this interface. The primary structure is reshaped by dragging individual walls forward or backward, which extends the floor, ceiling and adjoining walls to follow. The secondary openings can be translated across their wall and resized through their 27-sliced frame. The tertiary objects can be repositioned and selectively scaled through the start, end and destination planes of their scaling zones. Every operation provides immediate visual feedback, turning the static reconstruction into an editable digital environment.

% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\subsection{User Interface}
\label{sec:8_ui}
 
The scene is presented in the same layout as it was captured, but now populated with the dynamic openings and objects rather than static geometry. Each element can be edited individually. Selecting an object exposes visual gizmos for the direct manipulations, such as the handles that translate a wall, drag an opening across its surface or move the scaling planes of an object, so that the deformation can be performed by dragging directly in the 3D view. Alongside the gizmos, the same operations are available as definable parameters, letting a target wall position, opening size or scaling factor be entered as an exact value when precision matters. Combining direct gizmo manipulation with numeric parameters makes both quick rearrangement and precise editing possible within a single interface.

% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:8_experiments}

The dynamification is evaluated at the object level and at the scene level. The object-level results (Section~\ref{sec:8_exp_object}) compare the three scaling strategies on individual objects, and the scene-level results (Section~\ref{sec:8_exp_scene}) show complete dynamic environments in which those objects, together with the structural shell and the openings, can all be manipulated. The object-level experiments use both high-quality models with well-defined repeating structures and objects reconstructed and completed through Chapter~\ref{ch:7_Object_Completion}, while the scene-level experiments use the completed V-Scan~\cite{vermandere_synthetic_2026} and Matterport3D~\cite{chang_matterport3d_2017} environments of Chapter~\ref{ch:6_Scene_Completion}, since these provide the reconstructed wall fragments with flattened textures required for opening detection. All objects are represented as $128^3$ \glspl{CSDF}, and all scaling is performed and rendered interactively in the Unity environment of Section~\ref{sec:8_recombination}.


% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\subsection{Object-Level Results}
\label{sec:8_exp_object}

Figure~\ref{fig:8_scaling_comparison} compares the scaling strategies on two clean objects, showing for each the complete object, the globally scaled version, the part-aware scaled version and the repeated version.

\begin{figure}[hbht]
 \centering
 \includegraphics[width=1\textwidth]{Scene_dynamification_scaling_comparison}
 \caption[Comparison of scaling strategies]{Two objects (rows) under each scaling strategy (columns): the complete object, global scaling, part-aware selective scaling, and repetition-based scaling. Part-aware scaling keeps the proportions of parts outside the scaling zone but stretches the parts inside it, which repetition resolves by duplicating components instead.}
 \label{fig:8_scaling_comparison}
\end{figure}

Global scaling resizes the whole object uniformly, so every part distorts: chair legs thicken and structural members lose their proportions. Part-aware selective scaling improves on this by confining the deformation to the scaling zone, so parts outside the zone, such as the legs of a chair, keep their original proportions while only the targeted region changes. Inside the zone, however, the selected parts are still stretched, and for repeating structures this produces the same elongation that global scaling causes elsewhere. The repetition strategy resolves this by preserving the proportions of the parts within the zone: each repeatable component is stretched by at most a factor of $1.5$ before a new repetition is inserted, so the components stay close to their original size while the region still reaches the target length. This works best for straight parts, where successive repetitions can be placed seamlessly along the scaling axis; small deviations at the seams are absorbed by the Marching Cubes meshing step, which always produces a watertight surface.

\begin{figure}[hbht]
 \centering
 \includegraphics[width=1\textwidth]{Scene_dynamification_matterport_object_scaling}
 \caption[Scaling of reconstructed Matterport3D objects]{Three objects completed from Matterport3D scans (rows): the completed object, the selectively scaled version, and the repeated version.}
 \label{fig:8_matterport_object_scaling}
 \vspace{-10pt}
\end{figure}

The same behaviour holds on reconstructed objects. Figure~\ref{fig:8_matterport_object_scaling} shows three objects completed from Matterport3D scans, each with its selectively scaled and repeated versions. As with the clean models, selective scaling preserves the proportions outside the zone while repetition keeps the repeating elements consistent inside it, confirming that the method transfers from optimised geometry to the noisier completed objects of Chapter~\ref{ch:7_Object_Completion}.



These results confirm the structure-aware ordering of the strategies: global scaling is the least faithful, selective scaling preserves proportions outside the deformed region, and repetition is required to keep repeating structure consistent inside it. Two limitations carry over from the underlying representation. The $128^3$ \gls{CSDF} resolution trades detail against memory, so fine geometry can be lost during interpolation or resampling, and the convex decomposition is geometry-based, so its parts do not always align with semantic components, which occasionally places scaling-zone boundaries at non-intuitive locations~\cite{vermandere_adaptive_2026}.

The results are very good across both scenes. This is partly due to the simple geometry of the V-Scan environments, which are essentially boxes with clearly defined edges and cleanly delineated windows and doors, making the openings straightforward to detect and cut out. It is also due to the objects being synthetic, so they are clean, well-structured and mostly symmetrical, which suits both the convex decomposition and the repetition-based scaling. We therefore treat V-Scan as the best-case scenario for the pipeline.

\begin{figure}[hbht]
 \centering
 \includegraphics[width=1\textwidth]{Scene_dynamification_matterport_scenes}

Figure~\ref{fig:8_matterport_scenes} shows two Matterport3D scans alongside their complete dynamification, with the objects separated and the windows and doors recovered as movable elements. Combined with the draggable structural shell, the result is a scene whose primary, secondary and tertiary elements can all be manipulated after reconstruction.

The detection behaves reliably for closed openings, but a few failure modes appear. Open doors are difficult to detect, because the corridor visible behind them is reconstructed in a distorted view that does not present as a clean opening on the wall plane. The regions inpainted to fill object occlusions do not always have clear borders, so the detected polygons can pick up messy boundaries where the inpainted texture blends into the surrounding wall. Some built-in elements such as fitted cabinets are identified as doors, which is technically correct given their appearance but may not always be the intended interpretation. These three cases, open doors, occlusion-inpainted regions with unclear borders, and ambiguous built-in elements, are the main sources of scene-level error and stem from the planarity assumption of Chapter~\ref{ch:6_Scene_Completion} and the quality of the flattened wall texture.

Once the elements are separated, the scene can be freely rearranged. Figure~\ref{fig:8_matterport_variations} shows two alternative layouts of each of the same two scenes, with objects moved and scaled and windows and doors repositioned along their walls. The same reconstructed scene therefore supports many distinct configurations, which is the central goal of the dynamification stage.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:8_conclusion}

This chapter presented the dynamification stage that converts the static reconstructions of Chapters~\ref{ch:6_Scene_Completion} and~\ref{ch:7_Object_Completion} into a single interactive \gls{DRM}, acting on all three classes of building element. The primary structural shell is made draggable, the secondary windows and doors are detected in the flattened wall textures, separated as dynamic framed openings and re-inpainted behind, and the tertiary objects are converted into \glspl{CSDF}, decomposed into convex parts, and selectively scaled, either by jointly interpolating the signed distance, colour and part indices within a user-defined zone or, for repeating structures, by duplicating modular components, so that overall dimensions adapt while individual parts keep their proportions~\cite{vermandere_adaptive_2026}.

The qualitative results show that selective scaling preserves proportions far better than global scaling and that selective tiling is necessary to keep repeated structures coherent under large deformations, while the scene-level results demonstrate complete environments with separable openings and in-place scalable furniture rendered interactively in Unity~\cite{zhou_real-time_2008}. The remaining limitations, namely the voxel-resolution trade-off, the geometry-only part decomposition and the dependence on planar, well-textured walls for opening detection, define the directions for the future work discussed in Chapter~\ref{ch:conclusion}. With this stage, the pipeline closes the loop set out in Chapter~\ref{ch:introduction}: a partial scan is aligned, segmented, completed and finally made dynamic, yielding a complete and editable representation of the captured environment.
